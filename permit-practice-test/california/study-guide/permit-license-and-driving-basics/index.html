California Permit Study Guide: Getting Your Permit, the DMV Tests and Driving Basics

How a California instruction permit and license work: documents, steps and teen rules, the vision, knowledge and behind-the-wheel tests, then the basics every new driver must know about steering, signaling, the horn and headlights.

Most Californians drive with a noncommercial Class C license; commercial vehicles and motorcycles need other license classes. To get a Class C license you pass a knowledge test and a behind-the-wheel drive test, both based on the California Driver’s Handbook. This first lesson covers handbook Sections 1 to 5: getting your permit, the tests, and the basic skills the drive test checks.

Your license and ID card

  • REAL ID. Since May 2025, you need a REAL ID version of your license or ID card to fly within the country, get onto a military base or go into most federal buildings.
  • California issues driver’s licenses to all residents whatever their immigration status.
  • An ID card proves who you are but does not let you drive. It needs an identity document, residency documents and your social security number.
  • If you move, tell the DMV your new address within ten days. You do not get a new license automatically; a replacement costs a fee.
  • Driving with an expired license is against the law. Out of state when it expires? You can ask for a one-year extension (not available for limited-term licenses).

Getting an instruction permit

If you have never had a license from California or another state, you start with an instruction permit. You need proof of identity, two proofs of California residency (some exceptions apply), a legal full name document if your name differs from your identity document, and your social security number (exceptions may apply).

The steps:

  1. Fill out the Driver’s License & ID Card Application.
  2. Bring your documents.
  3. Pay the application fee, which is not refunded.
  4. Pass the knowledge test (or tests).
  5. Pass the vision test.

Extra rules if you are under 18

  • The minimum age is 15½, and you must have completed driver education.
  • A parent or guardian signs the application and accepts financial responsibility for you; with joint custody, both sign.
  • You cannot use the permit until you begin behind-the-wheel training with an instructor, who validates it.

Driver education and training are offered by driving schools licensed by the DMV and by some high schools. Instructors carry an instructor ID card, and you can ask to see it.

From permit to license

Everyone with a permit must practice next to a driver licensed in California and aged 18 or older (25 or older if you are a minor), sitting close enough to take over the controls, and then pass the drive test. Teens must also:

  • be at least 16;
  • have held a permit for at least 6 months (or have turned 18) before booking the drive test;
  • prove they finished both driver education and driver training;
  • log at least 50 hours of practice with a licensed driver aged 25 or older, ten of them at night.

The provisional license

Under 18, your license says provisional. For the first 12 months you may not drive between 11 p.m. and 5 a.m., or carry passengers under 20, unless a parent, guardian or another licensed driver aged 25 or older rides with you. Provisional drivers may never drive for pay or drive vehicles that need a commercial license.

There are exceptions, each requiring a signed note you carry with you: a medical need with no reasonable alternative (note from your physician with the condition and expected recovery date), school or a school activity (note from the principal, dean or designee), work (note from your employer confirming the job), or driving an immediate family member (note from a parent or guardian giving the reason, the person and the end date). A parent or guardian can also ask the DMV to cancel a teen’s license.

The tests

  1. Vision test. Everyone takes it. If you pass it wearing glasses or contact lenses, your license gets a corrective lenses restriction. If you fail, your eye doctor completes a Report of Vision Examination (DL 62).
  2. Knowledge test. Multiple choice. You get three tries before you must apply again, and minors who fail wait seven days (not counting the day of the test) before trying again. No handbook, phone or other aid is allowed. Some renewals can use eLearning instead, a self-paced course with quizzes.
  3. Behind-the-wheel drive test. The examiner may give two instructions at once, such as two turns in a row, to see whether you understand and follow both. Minors who fail wait 14 days before retaking it.

What to bring to the drive test

Your permit (or license), a licensed driver aged 18 or older (25 or older if you are a minor) unless you are already licensed, a vehicle that is safe to drive, and valid proof of insurance and registration. A rental car is fine only if your name is on the contract and the contract does not exclude drive tests.

The vehicle check before you drive

The examiner asks you to show these; if the vehicle fails, the test is rescheduled:

  • a driver’s window that opens and a windshield with a clear view (cracks can postpone the test);
  • at least two rearview mirrors, one on the left side;
  • working left and right brake lights;
  • tires with at least 1/32 inch of even tread (no donut spare);
  • at least one inch between the pressed brake pedal and the floor;
  • a horn that can be heard from 200 feet or more;
  • the parking brake (set it and release it), the hand-and-arm signals for left, right and slow or stop, the wipers, and seat belts that work and are worn.

An interpreter may help with the vehicle check but cannot ride along; only the examiner may, except for training, service animals and some law enforcement cases. Recording devices are not allowed: if one cannot be switched off, cover it. The test judges you, not your car’s technology, so driver assistance features such as automatic parking, lane keeping and adaptive cruise control must be off. Backup cameras and blind spot monitors are allowed, but you must still look in your mirrors and over your shoulder.

Your health and driving

Driving needs good vision (to see hazards in any light, judge distances and read signs), hearing (horns, sirens, screeching tires), alertness and good judgment. It is illegal to drive with headphones or earplugs covering both ears. Fatigue slows your reactions, and prescription or over-the-counter medicines can make you drowsy; it is your job to know how your medicines affect you. Doctors must report patients aged 14 or older whose condition, such as a loss of consciousness, may affect safe driving.

Steering

Keep both hands on the wheel whenever you can.

  • Hand-to-hand (push/pull): hold the wheel at the 9 and 3 positions (or 8 and 4), and keep your hands there without crossing them over the middle, even in turns.
  • Hand-over-hand: for slow turns, parking and recovering from a skid. Start at 8 and 4 o’clock, reach across to grab the far side of the wheel, release the other hand, then reach over the arm still holding the wheel and pull up.
  • One hand only in two cases: turning while backing up (hand at 12 o’clock, so you can look behind you), or while working a control that needs a hand off the wheel.

Signals, horn and headlights

Signal every time you turn, change lanes, slow down or stop, using your turn signals or, if they fail or bright sun hides them, hand-and-arm signals. Signal:

  • at least 100 feet before a turn;
  • before every lane change, and at least five seconds before changing lanes on a freeway;
  • before pulling to or away from the curb;
  • even when you see no one around;
  • when nearly through an intersection if you plan to turn just after it.

Turn the signal off when you are done. Cyclists may signal a turn by pointing an arm straight out in the direction they will go.

Use the horn to let others know you are there, to avoid a collision, and on narrow mountain roads when your view ahead is shorter than 200 feet.

Headlights help you see and be seen. Switch to low beams within 500 feet of an oncoming vehicle and when you are 300 feet or less behind another vehicle. Driving with only parking lights is illegal. Turn headlights on:

  • when you cannot see 1,000 feet ahead because it is too dark;
  • from half an hour after sunset to half an hour before sunrise (30 minutes each way);
  • whenever bad weather makes you use your wipers (rain, fog or snow), which means low beams by law;
  • when clouds, dust, smoke or fog make other vehicles hard to see;
  • on mountain roads and in tunnels, even on sunny days;
  • where a sign says so, and whenever the low sun makes your car hard to see.

Warning other drivers

If you see a crash or hazard ahead, warn drivers behind you: turn on your emergency flashers, tap your brake pedal lightly three or four times, or use the slow-or-stop hand signal. If your own vehicle has trouble, turn on the flashers (or the turn signals), get off the road if you can, and if you cannot, stop where drivers behind can see you, never just over a hill or around a curve. Call for roadside help and stay in the vehicle until it arrives.

Remember for the test

  • Permit at 15½ (under 18: driver education, parent signature). License at 16 after 6 months with a permit and 50 practice hours, ten at night.
  • Provisional license, first 12 months: no driving 11 p.m. to 5 a.m., no passengers under 20 without a licensed driver aged 25 or older.
  • Knowledge test: three tries; minors wait seven days to retake it. Drive test retake for minors: 14 days.
  • New address: tell the DMV within ten days.
  • Signal 100 feet before turning; five seconds before a freeway lane change.
  • Low beams within 500 feet of oncoming traffic and 300 feet behind another vehicle; headlights from 30 minutes after sunset to 30 minutes before sunrise, and whenever the wipers are on.
  • Nothing in or over both ears while driving.
