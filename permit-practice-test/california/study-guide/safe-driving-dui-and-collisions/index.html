The last part of the knowledge test material is about staying out of trouble: seeing what is around you, adapting to the road and the weather, protecting your passengers, and the strict California rules on alcohol, drugs, insurance and collisions. This lesson follows handbook Sections 8, 9 and 10.

Know what is around you

Keep your eyes moving and scan at least 10 seconds ahead, so you have time to react instead of making last-second moves. Think of the space around your car in four zones: ahead, beside you, your blind spots and behind you.

  • Ahead: tailgating hides the road and leaves no time to react if the driver in front brakes. Use the three-second rule for following distance. If someone cuts in close in front of you, lift your foot off the accelerator to open the gap again. Leave more room when a tailgater is behind you (keep your speed and course, then move right when it is safe so they can pass), and when following a motorcycle over metal surfaces such as bridge gratings or railroad tracks, or over gravel.
  • Beside you: do not linger in another driver’s blind spot or right alongside other cars, leave room for vehicles joining the freeway even when you have the right-of-way, keep space from parked cars, and look both ways at intersections even when cross traffic faces a red light or a stop sign.
  • Blind spots are the areas you cannot see looking ahead or in your mirrors, usually at the sides just behind you. Check them by turning your head (not your body or the wheel) to look out the side windows before changing lanes, turning at an intersection, merging, backing, leaving a parking space, parallel parking, pulling away from the curb and opening your door.
  • Behind you: check your mirrors and turn your head often, especially when changing lanes, slowing down, turning into a driveway or side road, stopping to park, pulling to or from the curb and backing up. It helps you avoid being hit from behind.

Road conditions

Night and glare

At night, use high beams on open country roads and dark city streets where they are legal, and drive so you can stop within the distance your headlights light up. Dim them for oncoming drivers. If oncoming lights are too bright, do not stare at them: shift your eyes to the right side of your lane and keep track of the other car out of the corner of your eye, and never answer with your own high beams. In rain, use low beams; never drive on parking lights alone. Remember that people walking or cycling and motorcyclists are much harder to see at night, that road work also happens at night, that your eyes need time to adjust after a brightly lit place, and that a single light coming toward you may be a bike, a motorcycle or a car with a burned-out headlight, so keep well to the right.

Against sun glare, keep the windshield clean inside and out, wear polarized sunglasses, keep space around you, make sure the visor works, watch hard for pedestrians, and avoid driving at sunrise and sunset when you can.

Skids

A skid happens when tires lose their grip. On ice and packed snow, especially when going too fast or downhill, slow down before curves and intersections, avoid fast turns and sudden stops, shift to a low gear before a steep downhill, and avoid ice patches, wet leaves, oil and standing water. If you start to skid:

  1. Ease your foot off the accelerator.
  2. Stay off the brakes.
  3. Turn the wheel the way the car is sliding.
  4. Aim to get one wheel onto dry pavement.

A locked-wheel skid usually comes from braking too hard at too high a speed, and the car slides whichever way you steer. With four-wheel ABS, press the brake firmly. With rear-wheel ABS (common on light trucks), ease off the pedal just enough for the front wheels to roll so you can steer, or stop braking and steer into the skid. With front-wheel ABS, take your foot off the brake to unlock the wheels, steer where you want to go and straighten as the car does. Without ABS, pump the brakes quickly until you are at a safe speed; if the pedal sinks to the floor, pump it, shift down to a lower gear or neutral, then try the parking brake. Wet brakes can be dried by pressing the accelerator and brake lightly together until they work again.

Rain, snow and slippery roads

Road Slow down by
Wet 5 to 10 mph
Packed snow half
Ice to 5 mph or less

Shaded spots stay icy longest, bridges and overpasses freeze first, and the first rain after a dry, hot spell is the most slippery because oil and dust have not been washed off yet. Turn on the wipers, low beams and defroster. In a heavy storm you may see no more than 100 feet ahead, and then 30 mph is the fastest safe speed. Carry chains in snow country.

Hydroplaning is when the tires ride up on water and lose all contact with the road, so a small turn, a brake tap or a gust of wind can start a skid. Prevent it by driving slowly, steering around standing water and slowing when you hear the tires slosh. If it happens, slow down gradually and do not brake.

Stuck in snow or mud? Shift to a low gear with the front wheels straight, press the accelerator gently without spinning the wheels, creep forward as far as you can, then back up as far as you can, and keep rocking back and forth until the car rolls free. In deep snow or mud, put boards or branches under the tires, only while the car is stopped.

Flooded roads can sweep you away, hide debris or a collapsed road, damage the car, and electrocute you near fallen power lines. You cannot judge the depth by looking: find another route. If you must cross, drive slowly and test your brakes afterward.

High winds are worst for trucks, campers and trailers. Slow down, hold the wheel firmly, watch for debris, do not use cruise control, and consider pulling over until the storm passes.

Fog or heavy smoke: better not to drive. If you must, go slowly with low beams (high beams reflect back as glare), never on parking or fog lights alone, so you can stop within the distance you see; increase your following distance, use wipers and defroster, avoid changing lanes or passing, and listen for traffic. If it gets too thick, pull off, turn on your flashers and wait.

Traffic breaks: an officer with lights on weaving across the lanes is slowing traffic on purpose, to clear a hazard or in fog. Turn on your flashers, slow gradually to the officer’s speed without slamming the brakes, keep your distance, never pass the patrol car, and do not speed up until the officer turns the lights off.

Protect yourself and your passengers

  • Seat belts are required for you and your passengers; you can also be ticketed if a passenger under 16 is not buckled. In a crash your body keeps moving until something stops it, and in a side impact you can be thrown against one door and then the other. Wear the shoulder belt across your chest and shoulder with little slack (never under your arm or behind your back) and the lap belt snug and low on your hips. Pregnant women keep the lap belt as low as they can, below the abdomen, with the shoulder strap between the breasts and beside the bulge.
  • Child seats: a child under 2 years old, under 40 pounds and less than 3 feet 4 inches tall rides in a rear-facing seat, which may never go in the front seat of a car with an active front airbag. Children under 8, or shorter than 4 feet 9 inches, ride in a child restraint in the back seat; they may ride in front only in special cases (no back seat, side-facing jump seats or rear-facing back seats, a seat that cannot be installed properly in back, a back seat already full of children aged 7 or younger, or medical reasons). From age 8, or once at least 4 feet 9 inches tall, a properly fitted safety belt may be used. Police and fire departments can check your installation.
  • Air bags: sit at least 10 inches from the air bag cover (measured from the center of the wheel to your breastbone), and passengers too; children next to a side air bag can be badly hurt.
  • Never leave a child aged 6 or younger alone in a car; someone aged 12 or older must watch them. Leaving children or animals in a hot car is dangerous and illegal: the temperature rises fast, even with a window cracked open.

Manage your speed

California’s Basic Speed Law: never drive faster than is safe for current conditions, whatever the posted limit. Your safe speed depends on the amount and speed of traffic, the road surface, people walking or cycling, the weather and congestion. Unless signs say otherwise, the maximum on most California highways is 65 mph, and 55 mph on two-lane undivided highways and for vehicles towing trailers.

When there are dangers on both sides, such as parked cars on one side and oncoming traffic on the other, give the most room to the worst danger, or deal with them one after the other: slow down and let the oncoming car pass, then move left to give a cyclist plenty of room.

Emergencies

  • Tire blowout: do not panic or brake suddenly. Hold the wheel with both hands, keep your speed if it is safe, ease off the accelerator gradually, correct the steering, look and steer where you want to go, then slow down and pull off when it is safe.
  • Wheels off the pavement: grip the wheel, take your foot off the accelerator, brake gently, check traffic behind you and steer back on carefully, without jerking the wheel, which could send you into oncoming traffic.
  • Overheating: in extreme heat watch the temperature gauge, avoid long stretches at high speed and switch off the air conditioning; in extreme cold the coolant may be low, and the defroster or a slightly open window keeps the glass clear.
  • Broken down on the freeway: pull onto the right shoulder, get out on the side away from traffic if you must leave the car, call 511 or use a call box, then get back in from the right side and stay belted in until help comes; the flashers are your call. If the shoulder is too narrow, there is a guardrail, or there is a safe spot away from the lanes, waiting outside may be safer. During commute hours, the CHP Freeway Service Patrol helps for free in some areas (a gallon of gas, a jump start, radiator water and hose tape, a tire change, a free tow to an approved spot), but it will not tow you home or to a repair shop, recommend shops, tow motorcycles or help after a collision unless CHP asks.
  • Stalled on railroad tracks: if a train is coming or lights are flashing, get out immediately and run away from the tracks at an angle, toward the train rather than away from it, then dial 911. If no train is coming, get out and phone the number posted on the crossing or on the metal box beside the tracks, give the crossing number and report the stuck car; after that, dial 911.

Distractions and phones

Common distractions: phones, navigation screens, children and pets, changing music, makeup or shaving. Phones are the biggest one, and holding a phone while driving is illegal. Adults may use a phone only in hands-free mode, and only when necessary: let calls go to voicemail, pull over safely to make one, never text or email while driving, and mount the phone on the windshield, dashboard or center console where it does not block your view, using only a single swipe or touch. Drivers under 18 may not use a phone or other wireless device at all while driving, except to call for emergency help.

Newer cars with self-driving features and driver assistance systems may react differently from a human driver.

Carbon monoxide from the exhaust is deadly and has no smell. Never start the engine in a closed garage. Tiredness, yawning, dizziness, nausea, headaches or ringing ears while driving can be signs of poisoning: have the exhaust checked.

Alcohol and drugs

California’s DUI laws cover alcohol and any drug that affects your driving, legal or not: medicines from a doctor or pharmacy count, and so do cough syrup and over-the-counter drugs. Read medicine labels. Your tolerance for alcohol also falls as you get older.

BAC limits

It is illegal to drive with a blood alcohol concentration (BAC) of:

  • 0.08% or more if you are 21 or older;
  • 0.01% or more if you are under 21, or if you are on DUI probation;
  • 0.04% or more in a vehicle that needs a commercial license, or when carrying a passenger for hire.

A BAC under the limit does not mean you are safe: almost everyone is affected below it, and you can be convicted of DUI without any BAC reading if you are impaired. Even one drink affects your driving. A drink means 1.5 oz of 80 proof liquor, 12 oz of 5% beer or 5 oz of 12% wine, and BAC falls by only about 0.01% for every 40 minutes between drinks. A smaller person reaches a higher BAC from the same drinks, and the handbook’s chart shows women reaching higher levels than men of the same weight.

Open containers and cannabis

Drinking alcohol or smoking or eating cannabis is illegal for the driver and for passengers in a moving vehicle. Alcohol or cannabis you carry must be sealed and unopened; an opened container goes in the trunk or somewhere passengers do not sit.

Tests, arrests and convictions

Driving in California means you agree to a breath, blood or urine test when an officer suspects DUI; you may have to give blood or urine even after a breath test, to check for drugs. Refusing means a one-year suspension or revocation. If you are arrested for DUI, the DMV must suspend your license (the Administrative Per Se law), the officer may give you a temporary license valid for 30 days, and you have 10 days from the arrest to ask for a DMV hearing.

A DUI conviction brings a one-year suspension or revocation, a DUI program, an SR 22/SR 1P insurance certificate, reissue fees and possibly an ignition interlock device, plus up to six months in jail, fines, impounding of your car, and civil lawsuits if you hurt or kill someone. DUI convictions stay on your record for 10 years, and new ones in that time bring bigger penalties. Teens aged 13 to 20 convicted of cycling under the influence can have their driving privilege suspended or delayed for one year.

Under 21

You may carry alcohol in a car only with someone aged 21 or older present, and only full, sealed and unopened (opened containers go in the trunk), unless you work for someone with an off-site liquor license. If you are caught with alcohol, your car can be impounded for up to 30 days, and a court can fine you and suspend your license for one year, or delay your first license by up to one year. An officer who suspects drinking can require a breath test: a BAC of 0.01% or more brings a one-year revocation and a DUI program, and 0.05% or more on a preliminary test can lead to further tests, arrest for DUI and suspension.

Insurance

You must carry proof of insurance whenever you drive (including the drive test) and show it to the others in a collision. Minimum coverage:

  • $30,000 for the death or injury of one person;
  • $60,000 for death or injury of more than one person;
  • $15,000 for damage to property.

For drivers under 18, the parent or guardian carries the financial responsibility. Driving uninsured and having a collision means a suspension of up to four years, whoever was at fault; you can get your license back for the last three years by filing and keeping an SR 22/SR 1P certificate. Buy insurance only from agents licensed by the California Department of Insurance; a low-cost program helps drivers who cannot afford it.

Collisions

The most common causes are distraction, unsafe speed, improper turns, ignoring right-of-way rules or stop signs and signals, driving in the lane meant for the opposite direction, and driving much faster or slower than traffic. When you see flashers ahead, slow down and pass carefully; stay away from crash scenes so help gets through.

If you are in a collision:

  • Stop. Leaving is hit-and-run, a serious crime.
  • Call 911 at once if anyone is hurt; if nobody is, move the cars out of traffic, then call.
  • Show your license, registration, insurance and address to the other driver, the police and anyone else involved.
  • If anyone is injured or killed, a report must reach the police within 24 hours (your insurer or representative may file it).
  • If you hit a parked car or other property, try to find the owner; if you cannot, attach a note firmly to it giving your name, address and phone number, and report it to the police.
  • If you hit an animal, call the humane society or police, and do not move an injured animal yourself.

Report to the DMV within 10 days (form SR 1) when the collision caused more than $1,000 of property damage or any injury or death, however minor, whoever caused it and even on private property. Each driver must file; the police will not do it for you, and failing to report means a suspension. Reportable collisions go on your driver record no matter who was at fault.

Remember for the test

  • Scan at least 10 seconds ahead; three-second following distance.
  • Skid: off the gas, off the brake, steer where the car is sliding.
  • Wet road: 5 to 10 mph slower. Packed snow: half. Ice: 5 mph at most. Only 100 feet of visibility: 30 mph at most.
  • Rear-facing seat: younger than 2, lighter than 40 pounds and shorter than 3 feet 4 inches; car seat in the back under 8 or under 4 feet 9 inches. Sit 10 inches from the air bag.
  • Basic Speed Law; 65 mph on most highways, 55 mph on two-lane undivided highways and when towing.
  • BAC: 0.08% (21 and over), 0.01% (under 21), 0.04% (commercial or for hire). Refusing a test: one year.
  • Insurance: $30,000 / $60,000 / $15,000. Police report within 24 hours if anyone is hurt; DMV SR 1 within 10 days over $1,000 or any injury.
