CDL Doubles and Triples Study Guide: Pulling Two and Three Trailers

Why doubles and triples are the least stable rigs on the road, how a converter dolly works, the order for coupling and uncoupling twin trailers, and the extra inspection and air brake checks.

The T endorsement lets a Class A driver pull double or triple trailers. Doubles and triples have more parts that can fail and are less stable than any other commercial vehicle, so the test focuses on smooth driving, correct coupling and careful inspection. This lesson follows Section 7 of the CDL manual and builds on the combination vehicles lesson.

Driving doubles and triples

  • Rollover. Steer gently and take corners, ramps and curves slowly. A speed that is safe for a straight truck or a single-trailer rig on a curve can be too fast for doubles or triples.
  • Crack-the-whip. A quick steering move grows with every trailer, and the last trailer is the one most likely to turn over. Steer smoothly at all times.
  • Look far down the road, so that any slowing or lane change can be gradual. Smoothness is what prevents rollovers and jackknifes.
  • Manage space. These rigs are long and cannot turn or stop suddenly. Leave more following distance, wait for bigger gaps before entering or crossing traffic, and make sure the sides are clear before changing lanes.
  • Bad conditions. Your drive axles pull a long train of dead axles, so on slippery roads, in bad weather and in the mountains you are more likely to skid or lose traction. Take extra care.
  • Parking. Avoid any spot you cannot pull straight through, and know how a parking lot is laid out before you enter it.
  • ABS on dollies. Any converter dolly made from March 1, 1998 onward must have antilock brakes, with a yellow lamp on its left side.

The converter dolly

A converter dolly (converter gear) is a coupling device with one or two axles and a fifth wheel. It lets a semitrailer be hooked to the back of a tractor-trailer, which makes a “double bottom” rig. The dolly’s tongue hooks into the pintle hook of the trailer in front.

Load order matters: put the heavier trailer first, right behind the tractor, and the lighter one at the rear.

Coupling twin trailers

1. Secure the rear trailer

If the second trailer has no spring brakes, drive close to it, connect the emergency line, fill its air tank, then disconnect the line: this sets its emergency brakes (provided the slack adjusters are adjusted). If you are in any doubt about its brakes, chock the wheels.

2. Put the dolly in front of the rear trailer

Release the dolly brakes by opening its air tank petcock (or with its parking brake control if it has spring brakes). If it is close, wheel the dolly by hand until it lines up with the kingpin. Otherwise use the tractor and first trailer to move it: back up to the dolly, hook it to the first trailer and lock the pintle hook, raise and secure the dolly support, pull it up close to the nose of the second trailer, lower the support, unhook it and roll it into line with the kingpin.

3. Connect the dolly to the front trailer

Back the first trailer up to the dolly tongue, hook the dolly on, lock the pintle hook and secure the dolly support in the raised position.

4. Connect the dolly to the rear trailer

  1. Make sure the rear trailer’s brakes are locked and/or its wheels chocked.
  2. Check the trailer height: it should sit slightly lower than the center of the dolly’s fifth wheel, so the trailer lifts a little as the dolly goes under it.
  3. Back the dolly under the rear trailer.
  4. Raise the landing gear just off the ground, so nothing is damaged if the trailer moves.
  5. Test the coupling by pulling against the kingpin of the second trailer.
  6. Look at the coupling: no gap between the upper and lower fifth wheel, and the locking jaws closed on the kingpin.
  7. Connect the safety chains, air hoses and light cords.
  8. Close the dolly air tank petcock and the shut-off valves (service and emergency) at the back of the second trailer.
  9. Open the shut-off valves at the back of the first trailer, and on the dolly if it has them.
  10. Raise the landing gear all the way.
  11. Push in the air supply knob to charge the trailer brakes, then open the emergency line shut-off at the back of the second trailer. If no air comes out, something is wrong and the brakes will not work.

Uncoupling twin trailers

The rear trailer: park the rig straight on firm, level ground and set the parking brakes; chock the second trailer if it has no spring brakes; lower its landing gear enough to take some weight off the dolly; close the shut-offs at the back of the first trailer (and on the dolly); disconnect and secure the dolly’s air and electrical lines; release the dolly brakes and its fifth wheel latch; then pull slowly forward until the dolly comes out from under the rear trailer.

The dolly: lower the dolly landing gear, take off the safety chains, set the dolly’s spring brakes or chock its wheels, release the pintle hook and pull slowly away.

Never unlock the pintle hook while the dolly is still under the rear trailer. The tow bar can fly up, injure someone and make re-coupling very hard.

Triples and other combinations

To build a triple: couple the tractor to the first trailer the usual way, then add each following trailer with a dolly, as for doubles. To take a triple apart, pull the dolly out from under the third trailer and unhook it, then uncouple the rest as you would a double. Other truck-trailer combinations have their own methods: learn the manufacturer’s or owner’s instructions for the rig you drive.

Inspecting doubles and triples

Follow the seven-step inspection and the combination checks (fifth wheels, glide plate, kingpin, air and electric lines, sliding fifth wheel, landing gear), then add these for each extra trailer:

Item Correct state
Shut-off valves at the rear of the front trailers OPEN
Shut-off valves at the rear of the last trailer CLOSED
Drain valve of the dolly’s air tank CLOSED
Air lines and glad hands Supported and properly connected
Spare tire on the dolly Secured
Dolly pintle eye In place in the pintle hook, hook latched
Safety chains Secured to the trailers
Light cords Firmly in their sockets

Air brake checks for doubles and triples

Do the normal combination checks, plus:

  1. Air reaches every trailer. Hold the rig with the parking brake or chocks, let the pressure reach normal, push in the red trailer air supply knob and apply the trailer hand brake. At the back of the last trailer, open the emergency line valve: air should hiss out. Close it, then do the same with the service line. If either line is silent, a shut-off valve on a trailer or dolly is closed. Air must reach the very back for every brake to work.
  2. Tractor protection valve. With the system charged and the engine off, fan the brake pedal: the trailer air supply control should pop out at the maker’s pressure, usually between 20 and 45 psi. If it does not, a leaking hose or trailer brake could empty the tractor’s air too.
  3. Trailer emergency brakes. Charge the system, check that the trailers roll freely, then pull the air supply control out and tug gently: the trailer brakes should hold.
  4. Trailer service brakes. With normal pressure and the parking brakes released, roll forward slowly and use the trailer hand valve: you should feel the trailer brakes.

Remember for the test

  • The last trailer is the most likely to turn over: steer gently.
  • Heaviest trailer first, lightest at the rear.
  • Shut-offs open on the front trailers, closed on the last one; dolly tank drain closed.
  • Never release the pintle hook with the dolly still under a trailer.
  • Check for air at the back of the last trailer before you drive.
