CDL Combination Vehicles Study Guide: Tractor-Trailers, Trailer Air Brakes and Coupling

What makes tractor-trailers harder to drive, how trailer air brakes and the tractor protection valve work, and how to couple, uncouple and inspect a combination step by step.

Combination vehicles (a tractor with a semitrailer, a truck with a trailer, doubles and triples) are heavier and longer than single vehicles and need more skill. Class A applicants take the combination vehicles knowledge test. This lesson follows Section 6 of the CDL manual; read the air brakes lesson first, because trailer brakes build on it.

Driving a combination safely

Rollover

Rollovers cause more than half of the deaths of truck drivers in crashes. Piling cargo high raises the center of gravity, and in a crash a rig with a full load turns over ten times as often as an empty one. Two habits prevent most rollovers:

  • Keep the load low and centered. A load that sits to one side makes the trailer lean. Spread the cargo out as much as you can.
  • Take turns slowly, including on-ramps and off-ramps, and avoid quick lane changes when loaded.

The crack-the-whip effect

A quick steering move gets bigger as it travels back through the trailers. This is called rearward amplification. A value of 2.0 means the last trailer tips over twice as easily as the tractor. Triples reach 3.5: compared with a five-axle tractor, their rear trailer overturns 3.5 times as easily. Many crashes involve only the trailer turning over.

So steer gently, look far ahead so you never need a sudden lane change, and keep a long following distance: at least 1 second for every 10 feet of vehicle length, plus 1 more second above 40 mph. At night, drive slowly enough to see what your headlights show while you can still stop or change lanes gently.

Braking: empty is harder than loaded

An empty or lightly loaded combination takes longer to stop than a loaded one: stiff springs and strong brakes give little traction, the wheels lock easily, the trailer can swing out and the tractor can jackknife. A bobtail tractor (no semitrailer) can be even harder to stop smoothly than a fully loaded rig. Leave lots of space and brake early.

Trailer skids (trailer jackknife)

When trailer wheels lock, the trailer swings around, most often when it is empty or light.

  1. Recognize it early by checking your mirrors every time you brake hard. Once the trailer leaves your lane, a jackknife is very hard to stop.
  2. Release the brakes so the trailer wheels grip again and the trailer lines up behind the tractor. Never use the trailer hand brake to straighten the rig: braking the trailer wheels caused the skid.

Railroad crossings

Low trailers can get stuck on raised crossings: lowboys, car carriers, moving vans, possum-belly livestock trailers, and a single-axle tractor pulling a long trailer whose landing gear is set for a tandem-axle tractor. If you get stuck, get out and away from the tracks at once, find the emergency information on the signpost or signal box, and call 911 with the crossing’s location (the DOT number if posted).

Turning wide

In a turn, the rear wheels cut inside the path of the front wheels (off-tracking). The longer the rig, the more it off-tracks, and the last trailer’s rear wheels cut in the most. Swing the front wide enough that the rear clears the curb and people, but keep the rear close to the curb so nobody can pass on your right. If you need extra room, turn wide as you finish the turn rather than swinging left before it.

Backing with a trailer

To back a trailer you turn the top of the steering wheel the opposite way from where you want the trailer to go, then follow the trailer once it starts turning. Back in a straight line when possible, or curve toward the driver’s side so you can see. Before backing, walk around and check side and overhead clearance; then back slowly, watch both mirrors, correct drift at once by turning the top of the wheel toward the drift, and pull forward to reposition when needed.

Combination vehicle air brakes

A combination has extra parts that control the trailer brakes:

Part What it does
Trailer hand valve (trolley valve, Johnson bar) Applies the trailer brakes only. Use it to test trailer brakes, never while driving (it can make the trailer skid) and never to park.
Tractor protection valve Keeps air in the tractor if the trailer breaks away or leaks badly. It closes by itself when pressure drops to between 20 and 45 psi, which sets the trailer emergency brakes.
Trailer air supply control On newer vehicles a red, eight-sided knob. Push in to supply the trailer, pull out to cut the air and set the trailer emergency brakes. It pops out on its own between 20 and 45 psi.
Service line (control or signal line, often blue) Carries air from the foot brake or hand valve; relay valves use it to apply the trailer brakes quickly.
Emergency line (supply line, often red) Fills the trailer air tanks and controls the trailer emergency brakes: if it loses pressure, the trailer emergency brakes come on and the tractor protection valve closes.
Glad hands Couplers that join the air lines. Clean the seals, press them together at a 90 degree angle and turn to lock. Use dummy couplers when no trailer is attached.
Trailer air tanks Filled through the emergency line. Drain each one every day, even with automatic drains.
Shut-off valves (cut-out cocks) At the back of trailers that can tow another trailer. Every one must be open except the pair on the rearmost trailer, which you close.

Crossed air lines send supply air into the service line, so the trailer tanks never fill and spring brakes will not release. On an old trailer without spring brakes, you could drive off with no trailer brakes at all. That is why you always test the trailer brakes before driving.

Trailers and converter dollies built before 1975 do not have to have spring brakes. Their emergency brakes run on the air stored in the trailer tank, and they have no parking brake: once that air leaks away there are no brakes, so always chock the wheels when you park such a trailer. Trailers and dollies built on or after March 1, 1998 must have ABS, with a yellow malfunction lamp on the left side. ABS helps you keep control but does not let you drive faster or closer.

Coupling a tractor-semitrailer

The manual lists 16 steps. In short:

  1. Inspect the fifth wheel: no damaged or missing parts, mounted securely, plate greased, tilted down toward the rear, jaws open, the safety unlocking handle set to lock automatically, sliding fifth wheel locked, kingpin not bent or broken.
  2. Inspect the area and chock the trailer wheels (or make sure its spring brakes are on); check that any cargo is secured.
  3. Line the tractor up straight with the trailer. Backing under it at an angle can shove the trailer sideways and snap the landing gear.
  4. Back slowly until the fifth wheel just touches the trailer.
  5. Secure the tractor: parking brake on, transmission in neutral.
  6. Check trailer height: the tractor should lift the trailer slightly as it backs under. Too low and you hit the trailer nose; too high and it may not couple. Kingpin and fifth wheel aligned.
  7. Connect the air lines, emergency to emergency and service to service, supported so they cannot be crushed.
  8. Supply air to the trailer (push in the air supply knob), wait for normal pressure, check for crossed lines, then with the engine off apply and release the trailer brakes and listen for them.
  9. Lock the trailer brakes by pulling the air supply knob out.
  10. Back under the trailer in the lowest reverse gear until the kingpin locks.
  11. Test the connection: landing gear slightly off the ground, pull forward gently against the locked trailer brakes.
  12. Secure the vehicle: neutral, parking brakes on, engine off, key in your pocket before you go under the trailer.
  13. Inspect the coupling with a flashlight: no space between the upper and lower fifth wheel, jaws closed around the shank of the kingpin, locking lever in “lock”, safety latch over the lever. If anything is wrong, do not drive.
  14. Connect the electrical cord and check all lines for damage and clearance from moving parts.
  15. Raise the landing gear all the way, starting in low gear, and secure the crank handle. Check clearance between the tractor frame and the landing gear, and between the tires and the trailer nose.
  16. Remove and store the wheel chocks.

Uncoupling

  1. Park on ground that can carry the trailer, with the tractor lined up straight.
  2. Lock the trailer brakes, back up gently to ease pressure on the locking jaws, and set the parking brakes while pushing against the kingpin.
  3. Chock the trailer wheels if it has no spring brakes, or if you are not sure.
  4. Lower the landing gear until it touches firmly; with a loaded trailer, give the crank a few extra turns in low gear to take some weight off the tractor (without lifting the trailer off the fifth wheel).
  5. Disconnect the air lines (onto dummy couplers) and hang the electrical cable plug-down.
  6. Unlock the fifth wheel, keeping your legs clear of the rear tractor wheels.
  7. Pull forward until the fifth wheel is out from under the trailer, but stop with the tractor frame still under it in case the landing gear sinks.
  8. Secure the tractor, check that the ground and the landing gear are holding the trailer, then drive clear.

Inspecting a combination

Use the seven-step inspection, plus these checks:

  • Lower fifth wheel: secure, greased, nothing missing, no gap between upper and lower fifth wheel, jaws around the kingpin shank (not its head), release arm seated and safety latch engaged.
  • Upper fifth wheel: glide plate secure, kingpin undamaged. Sliding fifth wheel: locking pins in place, no air leaks, not so far forward that the frame hits the landing gear or the cab hits the trailer in turns.
  • Lines: electrical cord plugged in and secured; air lines connected, not leaking, with slack for turns.
  • Landing gear: fully up, undamaged, crank handle secured.

Combination brake checks, in addition to the air brake checks:

  1. Air reaches every trailer: with air supplied and the hand brake on, open the emergency line valve at the back of the last trailer and listen for air, close it, then do the same with the service line. No air means a shut-off valve is closed somewhere.
  2. Tractor protection valve: with the engine off, fan the brake pedal; the air supply knob should pop out at the maker’s pressure, usually between 20 and 45 psi.
  3. Trailer emergency brakes: charge the system, check the trailer rolls freely, then pull the air supply knob and tug gently: the trailer brakes should hold.
  4. Trailer service brakes: roll forward slowly and apply the hand valve. You should feel the trailer brakes; in normal driving you still brake with the foot pedal.

Common mistakes

  • Using the trailer hand valve to stop, to straighten a skid or to park.
  • Backing under a trailer at an angle, or with the trailer too high or too low.
  • Driving with the landing gear only part way up.
  • Leaving the shut-off valves of the last trailer open, or a middle one closed.
  • Treating an empty rig as easier to stop than a loaded one.
