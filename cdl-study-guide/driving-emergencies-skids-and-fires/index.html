CDL General Knowledge: Emergencies, ABS, Skids, Crashes and Fires

What to do when things go wrong: evasive steering, the two emergency braking methods, failed brakes and tires, what ABS can and cannot do, how to recover from skids, what to do at a crash, and how to fight a truck fire.

A traffic emergency is two vehicles about to collide; a vehicle emergency is a tire, brake or other vital part failing. Safe habits prevent most of them, but when one happens, what you do in the next few seconds decides the outcome. This fifth General Knowledge lesson covers Sections 2.17 to 2.21 of the CDL manual.

Steering to avoid a crash

When there is not enough room to stop, swerving may be the better choice: in most cases you can steer around an obstacle sooner than you can brake to a stop for it, although top-heavy vehicles and tractors with several trailers may roll over.

  • Both hands on the wheel, all the time: it is the only way to be sure they are there when you need a quick turn.
  • Do not brake while turning: wheels lock easily in a turn and you may skid out of control.
  • Turn only as much as needed to clear the obstacle; the sharper the turn, the higher the risk of a skid or rollover.
  • Be ready to counter-steer, turning back the other way once you are past. Think of the steer and the counter-steer as one single move, or you will be too slow.

Which way to steer

If an oncoming driver drifts into your lane, move right: when that driver notices, they will most likely swerve back into their own lane. If something blocks your lane, your mirrors tell you which lane is free. A clear shoulder is often the best way out, since a car may be passing on your left but rarely on the shoulder. Blocked on both sides? Moving right is usually best, because you will not push anyone into oncoming traffic and a head-on crash.

Leaving the road

Going off the road can be less risky than hitting another vehicle, and most shoulders can carry a heavy vehicle.

  • Stay off the brakes until you are down to about 20 mph, then brake very gently so you do not skid on the loose surface.
  • If possible, leave the wheels on one side on the pavement; it helps you keep control.
  • With a clear shoulder, ride it all the way to a stop, then signal and check your mirrors before rejoining the road.
  • If you have to get back on before stopping, grip the wheel and turn sharply enough to get straight back on, rather than edging back gradually, where the tires can catch and throw you out of control. As soon as both front tires are back on the pavement, counter-steer: one quick steer-counter-steer motion.

Stopping quickly

When someone pulls out in front of you and you have room to stop, braking is right, but brake so the vehicle stays straight and you can still steer.

  • Controlled braking: press the brakes as firmly as possible while the wheels keep turning, with only very small steering movements. If you need a bigger steering correction or the wheels lock, ease off, then brake again as soon as you can.
  • Stab braking: brake fully, release when the wheels lock, and brake fully again as soon as they roll. It can take up to one second for locked wheels to start rolling again; brake before that and the vehicle will not straighten out.
  • Never jam the pedal to the floor: it just keeps the wheels locked and you skid with no control.

Worked example: a stab-braking sequence. A car cuts in and you have room to stop, but not much. You press the brake fully and the wheels lock. You release at once. The wheels begin to roll again, which can take up to one second. Only then do you press fully a second time. If you press again before the wheels roll, they simply stay locked and the vehicle will not straighten out. Repeat the cycle until you have stopped, and keep your hands steady on the wheel throughout.

Brake failure

Well-kept brakes rarely fail. Hydraulic brakes usually fail for two reasons: loss of hydraulic pressure, or fade on long hills. (Air brakes are covered in the air brakes lesson.)

When pressure is lost, the pedal feels spongy or sinks to the floor. Then:

  • Downshift to slow the vehicle.
  • Pump the pedal; it may build enough pressure to stop.
  • Use the parking brake, which is separate from the hydraulic system. Hold the release button or lever as you apply it so you can control the force and keep the wheels from locking.
  • Look for an escape route while slowing: an open field, a side street or an escape ramp. Turning uphill slows you well; once stopped, put it in a low gear, set the parking brake, and if needed let it roll back against something that will hold it.

On a long downgrade, the right speed and braking technique almost always prevent failure. If the brakes do fail there, you need something outside the vehicle to stop it. Use an escape ramp if there is one; signs announce them, and they are usually a few miles below the top of the grade. Some use deep soft gravel, others run uphill, and some do both. Skipping the ramp makes a serious crash far more likely. With no ramp, take the least dangerous way out, for example an empty field, or a side road that runs level or climbs, and do it right away: every second you wait, you go faster.

Tire failure

Recognize it fast. A loud bang may be your tire even if it seems to come from elsewhere, since the vehicle can take a few seconds to react; assume it is yours. Heavy thumping or vibration may be the only sign of a flat rear tire. Heavy steering points to a front tire, and a rear failure can make the vehicle fishtail, though duals usually prevent that.

Then:

  1. Hold the wheel firmly with both hands: a failed front tire can wrench it out of your grip.
  2. Stay off the brake unless you are about to hit something. Braking can cost you control. Let the vehicle slow, then brake very gently, pull off and stop.
  3. Check every tire once stopped, even if the vehicle seems fine. A failed dual may only show when you look at it.

Antilock braking systems (ABS)

ABS is a computer system that keeps the wheels from locking when you brake hard. Sensors spot a wheel about to lock and an electronic control unit (ECU) reduces the pressure on it, far faster than any driver could, to give maximum braking without lockup. The rest of the time the brakes work normally. ABS adds to your brakes; it does not make them stronger or weaker, and it does not necessarily shorten stopping distance. Its value is control: you can keep steering while braking hard and avoid skids and jackknifes caused by over-braking.

Which vehicles have it

The DOT requires ABS on air-braked truck tractors made from March 1, 1997; on all other air-braked trucks, buses, trailers and converter dollies made from March 1, 1998; and on hydraulic-braked trucks and buses rated at 10,000 lbs GVWR or more made from March 1, 1999. Plenty of older vehicles were fitted with it anyway.

The yellow malfunction lamp is on the dashboard of tractors, trucks and buses, and on the left side of trailers at the front or rear corner (dollies from March 1, 1998 have one on the left side). On newer vehicles it lights at start-up as a bulb check and goes out quickly; on older ones it may stay on until you pass five mph. If it stays on after the check, or comes on while driving, you may have lost ABS on one or more wheels: you still have normal brakes, so drive normally and get it serviced soon. On older trailers, look underneath for the ECU and wheel speed sensor wires to tell whether ABS is fitted.

Braking with ABS

  • Brake as you always have: only as hard as you need to stop safely and keep control, whether ABS is on the tractor, the trailer or both.
  • Watch the tractor and trailer as you slow and ease off the brakes, if it is safe, to stay in control. With ABS only on the tractor, you keep steering and jackknifes are less likely, but ease off if the trailer starts to swing out. With ABS only on the trailer, it is less likely to swing, but ease off if you lose steering or the tractor starts to jackknife.
  • One exception: if every axle of your straight truck or combination has ABS that works, you may brake fully in an emergency stop.

ABS will not let you drive faster, follow closer or take less care. It does not stop skids from spinning the drive wheels or taking a turn too fast, and it does not make up for bad brakes or poor maintenance. Drive so you never need it; if you do, it can help prevent a serious crash.

Skids

A skid happens when the tires lose their grip, in one of four ways: over-braking (locking the wheels, or using the retarder on a slippery road), over-steering (asking the vehicle to turn more sharply than it is able to), over-acceleration (spinning the drive wheels), and driving too fast, which causes most serious skids.

Drive-wheel skids

The most common skid is the rear wheels losing traction from too much braking or power. A power skid, usually on ice or snow, stops when you take your foot off the accelerator (on a very slippery road, also push in the clutch so the engine does not hold the wheels back). When the rear drive wheels lock under braking, they have less grip than rolling wheels and slide sideways: a bus or straight truck spins out, and with a trailer, the trailer can push the tractor sideways into a sudden jackknife.

To recover from a drive-wheel braking skid:

  1. Stop braking, so the rear wheels roll again and stop sliding.
  2. Counter-steer: as the vehicle comes back on course it keeps turning, so turn the wheel quickly the other way or you will skid the opposite direction.

Staying off the brake, steering quickly, pushing in the clutch and counter-steering all take practice, best done on a large driving range or skid pad.

Front-wheel skids

Usually caused by driving too fast, but also by worn front tires or cargo that leaves too little weight on the front axle. The front keeps going straight however much you turn the wheel, and on a very slippery road you may not make a curve. The only cure is to slow down: stop turning or braking so hard, and lose speed as fast as you can without skidding.

At a crash

If you are in a crash and not badly hurt, act to prevent more damage and injuries in three steps:

  1. Protect the area. If your vehicle was involved, move it to the side of the road if you can. If you stop to help, park away from the scene, which emergency vehicles will need. Turn on your flashers and set out reflective triangles where drivers will see them in time.
  2. Notify the authorities. With a phone or CB, call for help before you get out. Otherwise, protect the scene first, then call or send someone, giving the exact location.
  3. Care for the injured. When someone qualified is already giving aid, stay out of the way unless asked. Otherwise, do not move a badly injured person unless fire or traffic makes it necessary, stop heavy bleeding with direct pressure on the wound, and keep the person warm.

Truck fires

Causes and prevention

Fires start after crashes (spilled fuel, flares used carelessly), in tires (under-inflated tires, duals that touch), in the electrical system (short circuits from damaged insulation, loose connections), from fuel (smoking, careless fueling, loose fuel connections) and in cargo (flammable cargo, cargo badly sealed or loaded, poor ventilation).

To prevent them, inspect the tires, the cargo and the electrical, fuel and exhaust systems, and make sure the extinguisher is charged. At every stop on a trip, feel the tires, wheels and body for heat. Follow safe procedures when fueling, braking and using flares, watch the gauges for overheating and the mirrors for smoke, and handle anything flammable with care.

Fighting a fire

Drivers who did not know what to do have made fires worse, so read the instructions on your extinguisher before you need them.

  1. Get off the road and stop somewhere open, clear of buildings, trees, brush, other vehicles and anything else that could burn. Never pull into a service station. Call emergency services with your location.
  2. Keep it from spreading. For an engine fire, turn the engine off as soon as possible and keep the hood closed if you can, spraying through the louvers, the radiator or from underneath. When the cargo of a van or box trailer is burning, leave the doors shut, especially with hazardous materials: opening them feeds the fire oxygen.
  3. Put it out. Stay as far back as you can, stand upwind so the wind carries the agent to the fire, aim at the base or source rather than the flames, and keep going until everything that burned has cooled. No smoke or flame does not mean it cannot flare up again.

Use the right extinguisher. A B:C extinguisher is for electrical fires and burning liquids; an A:B:C extinguisher also handles burning wood, paper and cloth. Water works on wood, paper and cloth, but never on an electrical fire (shock) or a gasoline fire (it spreads the flames). A burning tire must be cooled, which may take a lot of water. If you are unsure what to use, especially on hazardous materials, wait for the firefighters.

The classes of fire: A ordinary combustibles such as wood and paper, B gasoline, oil, grease and other greasy liquids, C electrical equipment, D combustible metals, which need special powders.

Easy to mix up

Looks alike How they differ How to tell them apart
Controlled braking and stab braking Controlled braking keeps firm pressure on while the wheels keep turning, with only small steering movements. Stab braking is full braking, then release when the wheels lock, then full braking again once they roll. Controlled means never lock up. Stab means lock up, release, repeat.
A drive-wheel skid and a front-wheel skid In a drive-wheel braking skid, you stop braking and counter-steer. In a front-wheel skid, the vehicle keeps going straight however you turn the wheel, and the cure is to slow down. Back wheels sliding: let go and steer. Front wheels sliding: slow down.
Hydraulic brake failure and a downgrade failure With hydraulic failure you downshift, pump the pedal, use the parking brake and look for an escape route. On a long downgrade, you need something outside the vehicle to stop you, so use an escape ramp if there is one. On a flat road, you have time to try the pedal and parking brake. Downhill, the ramp comes first.
Steering to the right in two different situations If an oncoming driver drifts into your lane, move right, because they will probably swerve back. If you are blocked on both sides, right is usually still best, because you will not push anyone into oncoming traffic. Both times the reason is the same: right keeps you away from head-on traffic.
Counter-steering when leaving the road and in a skid When you return to the pavement, you steer back sharply, then counter-steer once both front tires are on. In a skid, you counter-steer as the vehicle comes back on course. Either way, think of it as steer and counter-steer, as one single move.
ABS lamp on at start-up and lamp staying on A lamp that lights briefly is the bulb check and goes out. If it stays on after that, or comes on while you drive, ABS may be lost on one or more wheels. Short flash: all clear. Stays on: you still have normal brakes, so drive normally and get it serviced soon.
B:C and A:B:C extinguishers A B:C extinguisher is for electrical fires and burning liquids. An A:B:C one also handles wood, paper and cloth. Count the letters. More letters, more kinds of fire.
Water and the classes of fire Water works on wood, paper and cloth, but never on an electrical fire or a gasoline fire. Water for A only. Never for B or C.
Tire failure and brake failure reflexes With a failed tire you stay off the brake unless you are about to hit something, then brake very gently once slowed. With failed hydraulic brakes, pumping the pedal is one of the first moves. Tire trouble: hands tight, feet off the brake. Brake trouble: downshift and pump.

Remember for the test

  • You can usually steer around an obstacle faster than you can stop. No braking while turning; steer and counter-steer as one move.
  • Oncoming driver in your lane: move right. Off the road: no braking until about 20 mph.
  • Controlled braking: hard but no lockup. Stab braking: full, release on lockup, reapply when the wheels roll (up to one second).
  • Hydraulic failure: downshift, pump, parking brake, escape route. Downhill failure: use the escape ramp.
  • Tire failure: grip the wheel, stay off the brake, check all tires afterward.
  • ABS keeps control but does not shorten stopping distance; brake normally.
  • Drive-wheel skid: stop braking, then counter-steer. Front-wheel skid: slow down.
  • Crash: first protect the scene, then call the authorities, then help the injured.
  • Fire: off the road, away from buildings, never at a service station; B:C for electrical and liquid fires; no water on electrical or gasoline fires.
