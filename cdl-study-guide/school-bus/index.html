CDL School Bus Endorsement Study Guide: Danger Zones, Loading and Evacuation

How far the danger zones reach, how to set every mirror, the step-by-step loading and unloading routine, when you must evacuate and where to lead students, and the school bus rules at railroad crossings.

School bus drivers need the S (school bus) endorsement in addition to the passenger endorsement, and must pass the skills tests in a school bus. State and local rules cover much of school transportation, so procedures can differ where you drive: learn your state’s laws and your district’s rules too. This lesson follows Section 10 of the CDL manual.

Danger zones

The danger zone is the area around the bus where children are most likely to be hit, by another vehicle or by their own bus.

Where How far it can reach
In front Up to 30 feet from the front bumper; the first 10 feet are the most dangerous
Each side 10 feet from the left and right sides
Behind 10 feet behind the rear bumper

The left side of the bus is always treated as dangerous, because of passing cars.

Setting up your mirrors

Check every mirror before you drive, and have it adjusted if needed.

Mirror What it is for What you should see
Outside flat mirrors (left and right front corners) Traffic, clearances and students along the sides and behind Both sides of the bus, the rear tires where they meet the ground, and 200 feet (about 4 bus lengths) to the rear
Outside convex mirrors (below the flat ones) A wide view of each side Each full side up to where the mirror is mounted, the front of the rear tires on the ground, and at least one traffic lane beside the bus. Objects look smaller and farther away than they are.
Crossover mirrors (front corners) The area right in front of the bus and down each side to the door and front wheels, which you cannot see directly From the front bumper at ground level up to where you can see directly (the two views should overlap), both front tires on the ground, and everything between the front of the bus and the service door
Overhead inside mirror Students inside the bus The top of the rear window at the top of the mirror, and every student, including the heads of those right behind you

There are blind spots just below and in front of each outside mirror, directly behind the driver’s seat, and behind the rear bumper, where the hidden area usually runs 50 to 150 feet and can reach 400 feet on a long, wide bus. Use your outside mirrors to watch traffic coming into it, and check all mirrors in the same order every time so no child is missed.

Loading and unloading

More students die getting on and off a school bus each year than riding inside one. Use only stops approved by your school district, and never move a stop without written approval.

Approaching the stop

  1. Slow down and keep checking all mirrors for students, traffic and other hazards.
  2. Turn on the alternating flashing amber lights (if fitted) at least 200 feet, or about 5 to 10 seconds, before the stop, or as your state requires.
  3. Signal right about 100 to 300 feet, or about 3 to 5 seconds, before you pull over.
  4. Move as far right as you can on the traveled part of the road.

Stopping

  • Keep 10 feet or more between your front bumper and the students waiting at the stop, so they have to walk to you and you can watch them.
  • Shift to park (or neutral if there is no park position) and set the parking brake at every stop.
  • Turn on the alternating red lights once traffic is a safe distance away, and make sure the stop arm is out.
  • Check that all traffic has stopped before you fully open the door and signal students to come.

Loading

Students wait at a set place facing the bus and board only when you signal. Count them, and learn their names if you can; if one is missing, ask the others. They board slowly, one at a time, using the handrail, with the dome light on in the dark. Wait until everyone is seated facing forward, and check that no one is running for the bus. When a student outside is unaccounted for, secure the bus, take the key with you and search around and underneath it.

Leaving the stop: close the door, put the bus in gear, release the parking brake, turn off the red lights, signal left, check all mirrors again, let traffic clear, and move off when it is safe. At school, turn off the engine, take the key if you leave your seat, and supervise loading as your rules require.

Unloading on the route

Students stay seated until you tell them to get off. Count them as they leave and tell them to move 10 feet or more out from the side of the bus, to a spot where you can see all of them. Check the mirrors again: nobody may be near the bus or coming back to it. If a student is missing, secure the bus and look around and under it. If you miss a student’s stop, do not back up: follow your local procedure.

Students who cross the road should:

  1. Move about 10 feet out from the side of the bus, into your view.
  2. Walk to a point at least 10 feet ahead of the right corner of the bumper, staying away from the front of the bus.
  3. Stop at the right edge of the road, where you can see their feet; look in every direction, check the red lights are still flashing, and wait for your signal.
  4. On your signal, cross far enough in front of the bus to stay in view, stop at the left edge of the bus to look for your signal again, then finish crossing while watching for traffic.

Unloading at school

Turn off the engine and take the key if you leave your seat. Let students off in an orderly way and watch that they move away promptly. Then walk through the bus to look for students who are hiding or asleep and for anything left behind, and check the mirrors that nobody is coming back. Before leaving, close the door, fasten your seat belt, start the engine, engage the transmission, release the parking brake, turn off the red lights, signal left, check the mirrors and let traffic clear.

Special dangers

  • Dropped objects: a student who stops to pick something up near the bus can vanish from your view at the worst moment. Teach students to leave it, move out of the danger zone and get your attention.
  • Handrail hang-ups: clothing, bag straps or even body parts caught in the handrail or door have killed students. Watch every student get clear before you move.

Post-trip inspection

After every route or trip, walk through and around the bus looking for left-behind items, sleeping students, open windows and doors, damage or vandalism, and mechanical problems, especially the mirrors, flashing lights and stop arms. Report problems to your supervisor or school right away.

Emergency evacuation

Stay or evacuate?

Usually students are safest and easiest to control on the bus, unless staying puts them at risk. Recognize the hazard, contact your dispatcher if there is time, and decide quickly. Think about fire, leaking fuel, the chance of being hit, tornadoes, rising water, downed power lines, dangers outside the bus, injuries that moving could make worse, and spilled hazardous materials (sometimes staying inside is safer).

You must evacuate when:

  • the bus is on fire, or a fire threatens;
  • the bus is stalled on or next to a railroad crossing;
  • the bus could shift position and make things more dangerous;
  • a collision is about to happen;
  • a hazardous materials spill requires leaving quickly.

Where to take students

Situation Safe place
Any evacuation by the road At least 100 feet off the road, toward the oncoming traffic, away from flying debris if the bus is hit
Fire Upwind of the bus
Railroad crossing As far from the tracks as possible, toward any oncoming train
Hazardous materials At least 300 feet away, upwind
Tornado with no building nearby A ditch or culvert far enough that the bus cannot roll onto them, students face down with hands over their heads

Stay away from areas that flood quickly.

How to evacuate

Plan ahead: where possible, assign two responsible older students to each emergency exit, plus one to lead the group to the safe place, but explain the procedure to every student, because those helpers may not be aboard. In an emergency: choose the exits (doors, roof or windows), secure the bus (park or neutral, parking brake, engine off, key out, hazard lights on), tell dispatch where you are and what help you need, leave the radio microphone or phone hanging out of the window if it works, or send a passer-by (as a last resort, two older students) for help. Order the evacuation. Do not move a student who may have a neck or spinal injury unless their life is in immediate danger. Walk through the bus to make sure it is empty, take the emergency equipment, count the students at the safe place, set out warning devices and prepare information for the responders.

Railroad crossings

Passive crossings have no traffic control device: the decision to cross is entirely yours, so be especially careful. Active crossings have flashing red lights, with or without bells, and sometimes gates.

  • The round, black-on-yellow advance warning sign and the “X RR” pavement marking mean slow down, look, listen and be ready to stop. If there is a white stop line, keep the front of the bus behind it; if not, stop before the cross-buck.
  • The cross-buck marks the crossing and means yield to trains; a sign under it gives the number of tracks.
  • Flashing lights mean stop: a train is coming. Gates: stop when the lights start flashing and stay stopped until the gates are up and the lights stop. If a gate stays down after the train has passed, do not go around it: call your dispatcher.

Recommended procedure:

  1. Slow down (in a lower gear with a manual transmission) and test your brakes.
  2. Turn on the hazard lights about 200 feet before the crossing, check traffic behind you, keep right and pick an escape route.
  3. Stop between 15 and 50 feet before the nearest rail, at the point with the best view of the tracks. Put the bus in park or neutral and hold the service brake or set the parking brake.
  4. Turn off radios and noisy equipment, ask the students for silence, open the service door and your window, and look and listen.
  5. Check the signals again. At several tracks, stop only before the first one, then cross them all once you are sure no train is coming. Cross in a low gear without shifting. If a gate comes down while you are crossing, drive through it, even if it breaks.

If the bus stalls on the tracks, get everyone off and away immediately, moving at an angle away from the tracks and toward the train. Obey a police officer at the crossing; if signals seem broken and no officer is there, call your dispatcher. Do not cross until you can see far enough along the track to be sure nothing is coming. Before crossing, make sure there is room for the whole bus beyond the tracks: as a rule of thumb, the space should be the bus length plus 15 feet. If it won’t fit, don’t commit.

Worked example: does the bus fit beyond the tracks? The rule of thumb is the bus length plus 15 feet of clear space on the far side. Measure the bus in your head, add 15 feet, and look at the road past the tracks. If traffic is backed up so that the free space is shorter than that total, you do not cross, even if the gates are up and the lights are off. That is what “if it won’t fit, don’t commit” means.

Student management

Never deal with behavior problems while students are getting on or off: keep your eyes outside. Wait until those leaving are safely away, and pull over if you must. For a serious problem, follow your school’s procedures: stop in a safe place off the road, secure the bus (take the key if you leave your seat), stand and speak to the student calmly but firmly, and move them to a seat near you if needed. Never put a student off the bus anywhere except at school or their own stop; if you cannot drive safely, call a school administrator or the police.

ABS, strobe lights, wind, backing and tail swing

  • ABS is required on air-braked trucks, buses, trailers and converter dollies made from March 1, 1998 onward, and on hydraulically braked trucks and buses of 10,000 lbs or more gross vehicle weight rating built on or after March 1, 1999. Brake normally; in an emergency stop with ABS, never pump the brakes. If the yellow ABS lamp shows a fault, your regular brakes still work: have it serviced.
  • Strobe light: use the roof strobe (if fitted) whenever your visibility is limited, following your state’s rules.
  • High winds push on the side of the bus like a sail. Grip the wheel firmly, slow down or pull off and wait, and ask your dispatcher for advice.
  • Backing is strongly discouraged and never allowed with students outside. If you have no choice: post a lookout (who warns you of hazards but does not direct you), ask for quiet, watch every mirror and back slowly. Without a lookout, set the parking brake, stop the engine, take the keys and walk to the back to check. Pick students up before backing, and drop them off only after backing.
  • Tail swing: the rear of a school bus can swing out up to three feet in a turn. Check your mirrors before and during every turn.

Easy to mix up

Looks alike How they differ How to tell them apart
Amber and red lights Amber alternating lights go on at least 200 feet, or about 5 to 10 seconds, before the stop. Red alternating lights go on once traffic is a safe distance away, and the stop arm is out. Amber warns, red stops traffic.
Ten feet, four different ways Keep 10 feet or more between your bumper and waiting students. Unloaded students move 10 feet or more out from the bus side. Crossing students walk to a point at least 10 feet ahead of the right bumper corner. The danger zone is 10 feet at each side and behind. Ask who is moving. The bus, the student getting off, the student crossing, or the child nearby.
30 feet and 10 feet in the danger zone The danger zone reaches up to 30 feet in front, and the first 10 feet are the most dangerous. At the sides and behind, it is 10 feet. The front is longest, but the closest feet matter most.
100 feet, 200 feet and 300 feet An evacuation by the road means at least 100 feet off the road toward oncoming traffic. Amber lights, and the hazard lights before a crossing, come on about 200 feet ahead. A hazardous materials spill means at least 300 feet away, upwind. The farthest distance is for the hazard you cannot see or smell.
15 to 50 feet and bus length plus 15 feet You stop 15 to 50 feet before the nearest rail. Beyond the tracks you need room for the whole bus plus 15 feet. One is how far back to stop. The other is how much room you need ahead.
A gate that stays down and a gate that comes down on you If a gate stays down after the train has passed, do not go around it. Call your dispatcher. If a gate comes down while you are crossing, drive through it, even if it breaks. Before you enter, stay out. After you enter, keep going.
Passive and active crossings A passive crossing has no traffic control device and the decision is yours. An active one has flashing red lights, with or without bells, and sometimes gates. Passive: you decide. Active: a device speaks, and you still look and listen.
Flat, convex, crossover and overhead mirrors Flat mirrors show traffic and the sides. Convex mirrors give a wide view, but objects look smaller and farther than they are. Crossover mirrors show the area in front and down each side. The overhead inside mirror shows the students. Outside mirrors watch the world. The crossover covers what is in front. The overhead watches the children.
A lookout and a director when backing A lookout warns you of hazards behind the bus. A lookout does not direct you. The lookout tells you what is there. You decide whether to move.
Pumping the brakes and ABS In an emergency stop with ABS, never pump the brakes. If the yellow ABS lamp shows a fault, your regular brakes still work, and the system needs service. ABS: press and hold, do not pump. A lamp fault: brakes still work, get it serviced.

Remember for the test

  • Danger zone: up to 30 feet in front (first 10 the worst), 10 feet at each side and behind.
  • Amber lights at least 200 feet before the stop; stop at least 10 feet from the students.
  • Students walk 10 feet from the side, and cross at least 10 feet in front of the bumper.
  • Safe place: at least 100 feet off the road toward oncoming traffic; 300 feet upwind for hazardous materials.
  • Railroad crossings: stop 15 to 50 feet from the nearest rail; room for the bus length plus 15 feet beyond.
