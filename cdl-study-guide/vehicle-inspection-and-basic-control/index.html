CDL General Knowledge: Vehicle Inspection, Basic Control and Shifting

The first part of the General Knowledge test: the inspections every driver must do, the defects that put a vehicle out of service, and the basics of starting, steering, stopping, backing and shifting a heavy vehicle.

Every CDL applicant takes the General Knowledge test, whatever the license class. It covers Section 2 of the CDL manual, Driving Safely, and Section 3 on cargo. This lesson is the first of six that walk through it, starting with the vehicle itself: inspecting it, then controlling it.

Why you inspect

Safety comes first, for you and everyone else on the road. A defect you find in the yard is cheap; the same defect found by a breakdown on the highway costs time and money, and one found by a crash can cost lives. Federal and state laws require drivers to inspect their vehicles, and inspectors can check them too. A vehicle judged unsafe is put “out of service” until it is repaired.

Three kinds of inspection

  • Before the trip: the full inspection, done the same way every time so no step is forgotten.
  • During the trip: watch the gauges and use your senses (sight, hearing, smell, touch). At each stop, check tires, wheels and rims, brakes, lights and reflectors, the brake and electrical connections to a trailer, the coupling devices and the cargo securement. Truck drivers must check cargo securement within the first 50 miles, then again every 150 miles or three hours of driving, whichever is sooner.
  • After the trip: when the trip, the day or your tour of duty ends, inspect each vehicle you drove and report anything that affects safety or could lead to a breakdown. That report is how the carrier learns what needs repair.

What to look for

Tires

  • Air pressure that is too high or too low.
  • Worn tread: at least 4/32 inch in every major groove on front tires and 2/32 inch on the others, with no fabric showing through the tread or sidewall.
  • Cuts, other damage, tread separation, cut or cracked valve stems.
  • Duals that touch each other or rub the vehicle, tires of different sizes, or radial and bias-ply tires mixed together.
  • On a bus, recapped, re-grooved or retreaded tires are not allowed on the front wheels.

Wheels and rims

Damaged rims are a defect. Rust around wheel nuts can mean they are loose, so check their tightness, and check again a short time after a tire change. Missing clamps, spacers, studs or lugs are dangerous, and so are lock rings that are mismatched, bent or cracked. A wheel or rim that has been repaired by welding is not safe.

Brakes and steering

Look for cracked drums, shoes or pads contaminated with oil, grease or brake fluid, and shoes that are dangerously thin, missing or broken. In the steering, look for missing nuts, bolts or cotter keys, and bent, loose or broken parts such as the column, gear box or tie rods; with power steering, check the hoses, pump, fluid level and leaks. More than 10 degrees of free play in the steering wheel (about 2 inches at the rim of a 20-inch wheel) makes steering hard.

Suspension and exhaust

The suspension carries the load and keeps the axles in place, so broken parts are very dangerous. Defects include cracked or broken spring hangers or hangers that let the axle move, missing or broken leaves (a vehicle with one-fourth or more of the leaves in a spring missing goes out of service), shifted leaves that could hit a tire, leaking shock absorbers, damaged torque rods, U-bolts or other axle-positioning parts, damaged or leaking air suspensions, and loose, cracked or missing frame members.

A broken exhaust can let poisonous fumes into the cab or sleeper. Look for loose, broken or missing pipes, mufflers, tailpipes, stacks and mounting hardware, exhaust parts rubbing against fuel lines, tires or moving parts, and leaks.

Emergency equipment and cargo

You need fire extinguishers, spare electrical fuses unless the vehicle has circuit breakers, and warning devices for a stopped vehicle: three reflective triangles, or at least six fusees, or three liquid burning flares. Before each trip, make sure a truck is not overloaded and its cargo is balanced and secured; a hazardous materials load also needs the right papers and placards.

The seven-step inspection

The CDL vehicle inspection test asks you to point to or touch each item, name it, and tell the examiner what you check and why. The manual suggests a seven-step routine; the pre-trip inspection lesson shows the full skills-test checklist.

  1. Overview. As you walk up, look for damage, a lean to one side, fresh leaks under the vehicle and hazards around it (people, vehicles, low wires, branches). Read the last inspection report and sign it only if the defects listed were certified as repaired or as not needing repair.
  2. Engine compartment, with the parking brake on or the wheels chocked: oil, coolant and hose condition, power steering fluid, washer fluid, battery, automatic transmission fluid, belt tension and wear, leaks, and cracked or worn wiring insulation.
  3. Start the engine and check the cab. Parking brake on, gearshift in neutral (or park), then listen for odd noises. An ABS light should come on and then go out. Oil pressure should reach normal within seconds, air pressure should rise from 50 to 90 psi in 3 minutes or less and keep building to the governor cut-out (usually around 120 to 140 psi), and the warning lights should go out right away. Check the controls for looseness, sticking or damage, the mirrors and windshield, the emergency equipment and the seat belt.
  4. Engine off, lights on. Set the parking brake, stop the engine, take the key, and turn on the low beams and four-way flashers.
  5. Walk around. Check the lights from the front, switch to high beams, then turn on the parking, clearance, marker and identification lights and the right turn signal and walk around the vehicle, cleaning lights, reflectors and glass as you go. Check every wheel, tire, suspension and brake, the fuel tanks, exhaust, frame, spare tire and cargo securement. Lights and reflectors facing the front are amber; the ones at the rear are red.
  6. Signal lights. Back in the cab, turn the lights off, put on the stop lights and the left turn signal, then get out and check them.
  7. Start the engine and test the brakes. With hydraulic brakes, pump the pedal three times, then hold firm pressure for five seconds: the pedal should not move. Air brakes have their own checks, covered in the air brakes lesson. Test the parking brake by pulling gently against it in a low gear, then test the service brake at about five miles per hour: pulling to one side or an odd pedal feel can mean trouble.

If anything is unsafe, get it fixed: the law forbids driving an unsafe vehicle. Keep a copy of your inspection report in the vehicle for one day so the next driver knows what you found.

Basic control

Wear your seat belt, and set the parking brake whenever you leave the vehicle.

  • Starting off: do not roll back into whoever is behind you. With a manual transmission, partly engage the clutch before lifting your right foot off the brake, and use the parking brake as needed, releasing it only once the engine has enough power to hold the vehicle. Accelerate smoothly: jerky starts can damage the drivetrain and a trailer’s coupling.
  • Poor traction: in rain or snow, add power very gradually. If the drive wheels start to spin, ease off the accelerator.
  • Steering: both hands, on opposite sides of the wheel, holding firmly so a curb or pothole cannot pull it out of your hands.
  • Stopping: press the brake pedal gradually, with only as much pressure as the speed and the situation need, for a smooth stop. With a manual transmission, wait until the engine is near idle speed, then push the clutch in.

Backing safely

You cannot see everything behind you, so backing is always dangerous: avoid it when you can, and park so you can pull forward when you leave. When you must back:

  • Start from the best position for the move.
  • Look at your path first; get out and walk around to check the clearance at the sides and overhead.
  • Check the mirrors on both sides often, and get out again if you are unsure.
  • Back as slowly as you can, in the lowest reverse gear, so you can correct the steering or stop quickly.
  • Back toward the driver’s side, where you can watch the rear through your window, even if that means going around the block. Backing toward the right is very dangerous because you see much less.
  • Use a helper standing near the back where you can see them, and agree on hand signals first, especially the one for “stop”.

Shifting gears

The wrong gear means less control. Most heavy vehicles with unsynchronized manual transmissions need double clutching; synchronized transmissions do not.

To shift up: release the accelerator, push in the clutch and move to neutral together; let the clutch out; wait for the engine and gears to slow to the right rpm for the next gear; then clutch in and shift up, and finally let the clutch out as you press the accelerator. To shift down, do the same but raise the engine speed in neutral to match the lower gear. If you stay in neutral too long and the gear will not go in, do not force it: return to neutral, release the clutch, bring the engine speed up to match road speed and try again.

Know when to shift by engine speed (shift up at the top of the rpm range shown on the tachometer; some newer vehicles use progressive shifting, with higher shift points in higher gears) or by road speed (learn what speeds each gear suits). With practice you will also hear when to shift.

Downshift before you start down a hill, usually to a gear lower than you would need to climb it, so you can hold your speed without hard braking that overheats the brakes. Downshift before a curve as well, so you can use a little power through it and speed up as you leave it.

  • Multi-speed rear axles and auxiliary transmissions add gears, usually through a knob or switch on the gearshift lever. Shift patterns vary, so learn the one in your vehicle.
  • Automatic transmissions: select a low range on downgrades for more engine braking; the transmission will not shift above it unless the governor rpm is exceeded.
  • Retarders (exhaust, engine, hydraulic and electric) slow the vehicle so you use the brakes less, which cuts brake wear. The driver turns them on and off, and some can be adjusted. They can make the drive wheels skid when traction is poor, so turn the retarder off on wet, icy or snow-covered roads.

Remember for the test

  • Tread: 4/32 inch on front tires, 2/32 inch on the others.
  • Steering play: no more than 10 degrees (about 2 inches on a 20-inch wheel).
  • Warning devices: three triangles, six fusees or three liquid burning flares.
  • Air pressure: 50 to 90 psi within 3 minutes; governor cut-out around 120 to 140 psi.
  • Hydraulic brake check: pump three times, hold five seconds.
  • Cargo checks: within 50 miles, then every 150 miles or three hours.
  • Back toward the driver’s side, slowly, with a helper.
  • Downshift before hills and curves; retarder off on slippery roads.
