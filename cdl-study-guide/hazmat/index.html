CDL HazMat Endorsement Study Guide: Hazardous Materials Rules

The rules behind the hazardous materials test in plain words: who is responsible for what, how shipping papers and placards work, how to load and park, and how to handle a leak, a fire or a crash.

The H (hazardous materials) endorsement lets you haul loads that need placards. Getting it takes more than a knowledge test: you also give your fingerprints and go through a TSA background check, and the endorsement can be refused or taken away if TSA decides you are a security threat or you fall under one of the other disqualifications in Section 1.3.6. If you also drive tank vehicles, the X endorsement combines the tank and HazMat endorsements. This lesson follows Section 9 of the CDL manual.

The rules have three goals. Contain the material (safe packaging, loading and transport), communicate the risk (labels, shipping papers, emergency information and placards), and make sure drivers and equipment are safe. Officers who stop you may check your shipping papers, your placards, your endorsement and what you know about the load. Breaking the rules can mean fines and even jail.

Who does what

  • The shipper uses the regulations to settle each product’s shipping name, identification number, class, packing group, packaging, labels, markings and placards. The shipper packages, marks and labels the material, prepares the shipping papers, supplies the emergency response information and the placards, and signs a certification on the shipping paper that the shipment follows the rules.
  • The carrier checks that the shipper described, marked and labeled the shipment correctly before it moves, and refuses shipments that are not right.
  • The driver makes sure the material is identified, marked and labeled, turns down leaking packages, placards the vehicle when needed, delivers without delay, follows every special rule, keeps the papers and emergency information where they belong, and reports incidents that happen while the shipment is under the driver’s control.

You may accept the shipper’s certification about packaging unless a package is clearly unsafe or breaks the rules. Your employer may add rules of its own: follow them too.

The nine hazard classes

Every hazardous material belongs to one of nine classes, some split into divisions. Learn the numbers: placards and papers use them.

  • Class 1, explosives, in six divisions: 1.1 mass explosion (dynamite), 1.2 projection hazard (flares), 1.3 fire hazard (display fireworks), 1.4 minor explosion (ammunition), 1.5 very insensitive (blasting agents) and 1.6 extremely insensitive.
  • Class 2, gases: 2.1 flammable (propane), 2.2 non-flammable (helium), 2.3 poisonous or toxic.
  • Class 3, flammable liquids such as gasoline.
  • Class 4, flammable solids: 4.1 flammable solids, 4.2 spontaneously combustible (white phosphorus), 4.3 dangerous when wet (sodium).
  • Class 5: 5.1 oxidizers (ammonium nitrate) and 5.2 organic peroxides.
  • Class 6: 6.1 poisons (potassium cyanide) and 6.2 infectious substances.
  • Class 7, radioactive (uranium), Class 8, corrosives (battery fluid) and Class 9, miscellaneous (PCBs).

Two more groups have no class number: ORM-D (other regulated material, domestic), such as some medicines and food flavorings, and combustible liquids such as fuel oil.

Shipping papers

Bills of lading, shipping orders and manifests all count as shipping papers. After a crash you may be hurt and unable to speak, so firefighters and police rely on finding the papers fast.

Spotting the hazardous materials entries

When one paper lists both hazardous and ordinary goods, the hazardous materials must come first, or be highlighted in a contrasting color, or carry an X in a column headed “HM”. RQ goes in that column instead when the amount is a reportable quantity.

What the description must show

  • The basic description in this order: ID number, then the proper shipping name, then the class or division, and last the packing group in Roman numerals (sometimes after “PG”). For example: UN1263, Paint, 3, PG II. These items may not be abbreviated unless the rules allow it.
  • The total quantity with its unit, and the number and type of packages.
  • RQ for a reportable quantity, plus the substance name if the shipping name does not include it, and the technical name for any generic entry.
  • An emergency response telephone number, which is the shipper’s responsibility.
  • Page numbers when there is more than one page, with the total on the first page (“Page 1 of 4”).
  • The shipper’s signed certification.

Hazardous waste carries the word WASTE before the shipping name. An ordinary material may never be described with a hazard class or identification number.

Emergency response information

The shipper must also give the carrier emergency response information that can be used away from the vehicle: the description and technical name, health hazards, fire and explosion risks, first steps after an incident, how to handle fires, spills and leaks, and basic first aid. It may be on the shipping paper, on another document, or in a guidebook such as the ERG. Hand it to any authority that responds to or investigates an incident.

Where the papers go

Tab them or keep them on top of other papers. While driving, keep them in a pouch on your door or in plain view within reach with your seat belt fastened. When you leave the cab, put them in the door pouch or on the driver’s seat. The emergency response information stays with them.

Shippers keep copies for 2 years (3 years for hazardous waste). A carrier that only carries the load keeps a copy for 1 year.

Hazardous waste manifest

Waste travels with a Uniform Hazardous Waste Manifest, which names every shipper, carrier and destination along with each one’s EPA registration number. The shipper prepares, dates and signs it by hand, and so does every carrier. Treat it as a shipping paper, hand the waste only to another registered carrier or a treatment or disposal facility, and keep your signed copy after delivery.

The three lists

Before hauling a material, look for it on three lists, because some products are on one list and others on all three:

  1. The Hazardous Materials Table (49 CFR 172.101). Column 1 holds symbols, column 2 the proper shipping names (names in italics are not proper shipping names), column 3 the class or division or the word “Forbidden” (never haul a forbidden material), column 4 the identification number, column 5 the packing group, column 6 the labels, column 7 special provisions and column 8 packaging sections. In column 7, the numbers 1 to 6 mark a poison inhalation hazard.
  2. Appendix A, hazardous substances and their reportable quantities. Spills of these must be reported when they reach the reportable quantity.
  3. The list of marine pollutants (Appendix B). On the highway it matters for containers of 119 gallons or more. Bulk packages of a marine pollutant, and the vehicle, show the marine pollutant mark (a white triangle with a crossed-out fish), and the shipping paper says “Marine Pollutant”.

The six column 1 symbols: + fixes the shipping name, class and packing group even if the material does not meet the class definition; A and W mean the rules apply only for air or water transport (unless the material is a hazardous substance or waste, or for water a marine pollutant); D marks a name for domestic shipments and I one for international shipments; G marks a generic name that needs a technical name beside it on the paper.

Identification numbers have four digits and start with UN, NA (names used only in the United States and to and from Canada) or ID (air shipments). One number can cover more than one chemical. It appears on the shipping paper, on the package, and on cargo tanks and other bulk packaging, and the ERG lists them.

Labels and markings

Most packages carry a diamond-shaped label for their hazard class; when one will not fit, the shipper uses a tag or decal (common on gas cylinders). Several labels go close together near the shipping name. Markings can include the shipper or consignee, the shipping name and identification number, and words such as RQ, MARINE POLLUTANT, BIOHAZARD, HOT or INHALATION HAZARD. Arrows show which way is up on packages holding liquids. Compare the packages with the shipping paper, and if you do not know the material, ask the shipper to call your office.

Clues that a load is hazardous: a shipping name, class and number on the paper; an X or RQ entry; the kind of business (paint, chemicals, pest control, fireworks); cylinders and drums; labels or handling warnings on the packages.

Placards

Placards go on the outside of the vehicle and on bulk packages to show the hazard class. A placarded vehicle needs at least four identical placards: front, rear and both sides, readable from all four directions. Each is a diamond at least 250 mm (9.84 inches) on a side. Put them on before you drive; an incorrectly placarded vehicle may move only in an emergency to protect life or property.

Each placard must be easy to see from the direction it faces, level and reading left to right, at least three inches from other markings, clear of ladders, doors and tarps, clean and undamaged, and on a contrasting background. Slogans such as “Drive Safely” are not allowed. The front placard can go on the tractor or the trailer.

To choose placards you need three facts: the hazard class, the amount being shipped, and the total weight of all hazardous materials on the vehicle.

Table 1: any amount

These always need placards: explosives 1.1, 1.2 and 1.3, poison gas (2.3), dangerous when wet (4.3), temperature-controlled Type B organic peroxide (5.2), 6.1 poisons that are inhalation hazards in zone A or B, and radioactive packages with a Yellow III label.

Table 2: 1,001 pounds or more

Every other class (except for bulk packaging) needs placards only once the Table 2 materials on board total 1,001 pounds or more with packaging, added up across all your shipping papers: explosives 1.4 to 1.6, flammable and non-flammable gas, flammable and combustible liquids, flammable solids, spontaneously combustible materials, oxidizers, other organic peroxides, other poisons, corrosives and Class 9. Infectious substances (6.2) and ORM-D need no placard, and the Class 9 placard is not required for domestic transport.

  • DANGEROUS placard: you may use it instead of separate placards when your Table 2 load reaches 1,001 pounds and mixes two or more classes that need different placards, unless you loaded 2,205 pounds or more of one of them at a single place (that one gets its own placard). It is always optional.
  • INHALATION HAZARD on the paper or package means POISON GAS or POISON INHALATION HAZARD placards in addition to the class placard, for any amount.
  • A secondary dangerous when wet hazard also means the DANGEROUS WHEN WET placard for any amount.

Bulk packaging is a single container holding more than 119 gallons. It must be placarded even when only residue is left. Cargo tanks and other bulk packages also show the identification number in black digits 100 mm (3.9 inches) tall on orange panels, on placards, or on a white diamond. Portable tanks show the owner’s or lessee’s name and the shipping name on two opposite sides, with the number on each side and each end when they hold 1,000 gallons or more.

Loading and unloading

Set the parking brake first. Never use hooks or tools that could damage packages, keep the material away from heat, and never accept a leaking package: moving a vehicle that leaks hazardous materials is illegal. Brace every package so it cannot fall, slide or bounce, and do not open any package or move material between packages during the trip (you may empty a cargo tank).

  • No smoking near Class 1, 2.1, 3, 4 or 5 materials, and keep all fire away.
  • Cargo heaters: the rules usually forbid them with explosives, flammable gas and flammable liquids.
  • Closed cargo space for explosives, flammable solids and oxidizers, unless every package resists fire and water or is under a tarp that does.
  • Explosives: engine off, cargo heaters disabled and drained, no sharp points in the cargo space, a non-iron floor lining for 1.1 to 1.3, and no dropping, throwing or rolling. Do not accept damaged packages or ones with damp or oily stains, and transfer 1.1 to 1.3 between vehicles on a public road only in an emergency, with warning devices set out.
  • Flammable solids and oxidizers: fully enclosed or securely covered, dry if water makes them dangerous, ventilated if they can heat up on their own.
  • Corrosives: load breakable containers one at a time, upright, on a level floor; never load nitric acid above other products; keep charged batteries upright and protected.
  • Gas cylinders: upright in racks or boxes, or lying down only if built with the relief valve in the vapor space.
  • Poisons (2.3 and 6.1): never in the cab or sleeper, never with food for people or animals.
  • Radioactive (Class 7): Yellow II and III labels show a transport index; the total on one vehicle may not exceed 50, and the rules limit how close these packages sit to people, animals and film.

Some products must never share a cargo space: the segregation rules and the “do not load” table in the manual list them.

Cargo tanks

A qualified person must watch the whole time a tank is loaded or unloaded: alert, with a clear view, within 25 feet, aware of the hazards and the emergency steps, and allowed and able to move the tank. Close all manholes and valves before moving, however little is inside or however short the trip. For flammable liquids, run the engine only to power a pump and ground the tank before opening the filling hole until after it is closed. For compressed gas, keep the discharge valves closed except during transfer, unhook every hose before coupling, uncoupling or moving, and chock uncoupled trailers.

Driving and parking

  • Explosives 1.1 to 1.3: never park within five feet of the traveled road, or within 300 feet of a bridge, tunnel, building, crowd or open fire except briefly for things like fueling. Someone must always watch the vehicle; another person may watch it only on the shipper’s, carrier’s or consignee’s property. A safe haven is the one place you may leave it unattended.
  • Other placarded loads: park within five feet of the road only briefly when your work requires it, keep the vehicle attended on a public road or shoulder, stay 300 feet from open fires, and never leave an uncoupled trailer of hazardous materials on a public street.
  • The attendant stays awake in the vehicle (not in the sleeper) or within 100 feet with a clear view, knows the hazards and the emergency steps, and can move the vehicle.
  • No flares or fusees around a tank for flammable liquids or gas (loaded or empty) or a load of 1.1 to 1.3 explosives: use reflective triangles or red electric lights.
  • Routes: check for permits and route limits before you leave, avoid crowds, tunnels, narrow streets and alleys when placarded, and carry a written route plan for 1.1 to 1.3 explosives.
  • No smoking within 25 feet of a placarded tank for flammable liquids or gas, or any vehicle carrying explosives, flammable liquids or solids, spontaneously combustible materials or oxidizers.
  • Fueling with the engine off and someone at the nozzle at all times.
  • Fire extinguisher rated 10 B:C or more on the power unit.
  • Tires checked at the start of each trip and every time you park, with a pressure gauge.
  • Railroad crossings: placarded vehicles, any load of chlorine, and hazardous materials cargo tanks (even empty) must stop between 15 and 50 feet from the closest rail, and do not shift gears on the tracks.
  • Chlorine tanks carry an approved gas mask and a leak-control kit.
  • Drivers of 1.1 to 1.3 explosives must have the shipping papers, written emergency instructions, the route plan and a copy of FMCSR Part 397, and sign a receipt for them.

Emergencies

Responders use the Emergency Response Guidebook (ERG), indexed by shipping name and identification number, which is why the papers and placards must be right.

At a crash, keep people away and upwind, limit the spread only if it is safe, warn others, call for help, give responders the shipping papers and emergency information, and follow your employer’s instructions.

  • Fire: you may fight a minor truck fire to keep it from reaching the cargo, but do not fight a hazardous materials fire without training and gear. Feel the trailer doors first; if they are hot, keep them closed, since air feeds the fire.
  • Leaks: find the material from the papers, labels or package location. Never touch it, never sniff to find it, and never eat, drink or smoke nearby. Do not keep driving to find a phone or help; the carrier pays for every contaminated mile. Park, secure the area, stay there, and call or send for help, giving the helper written details: the emergency, your exact location and direction, your name, the carrier and its terminal city, and the shipping name, class and number if you know them.
  • Explosives: take them out before wrecked vehicles are pulled apart and set them down 200 feet or more away from the vehicles and from any occupied building.
  • Compressed gas: notify the shipper after any accident.
  • Poisons: treat them as possibly flammable; check the vehicle for stray poison before it is used again. Report damaged infectious-substance packages to your supervisor.
  • Radioactive: tell your dispatcher fast, do not touch or breathe the material, and keep the vehicle out of service until it has been cleaned and passed a survey meter check.
  • Corrosives: wash exposed vehicle parts with water.

Required notification

You or your employer must call the National Response Center, (800) 424-8802, when an incident causes a death, an injury needing hospital care, damage over $50,000, an evacuation of more than one hour, a major road or facility shut for an hour or more, any fire, breakage, spill or suspected contamination involving radioactive or infectious material, or a marine pollutant release over 119 gallons of liquid or 882 pounds of solid. Carriers file a written report within 30 days. CHEMTREC, (800) 424-9300, gives responders technical information about the material.

Remember for the test

  • Placards: at least four, 250 mm (9.84 inches), three inches from other markings.
  • Table 1 any amount; Table 2 from 1,001 pounds; DANGEROUS not allowed for 2,205 pounds of one class loaded at one place.
  • Papers: door pouch or within reach while driving, door pouch or driver’s seat when away.
  • Attend within 25 feet when loading a tank and within 100 feet when parked; no smoking within 25 feet.
  • Explosives: five feet from the road, 300 feet from bridges, tunnels, buildings, crowds and fires; 200 feet after a crash.
  • Railroad crossings: stop between 15 and 50 feet from the closest rail.
  • Total transport index on one vehicle: 50 at most.
