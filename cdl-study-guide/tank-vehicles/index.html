CDL Tanker Endorsement Study Guide: Liquid Surge, Baffles and Outage

Why tank vehicles handle differently, what to check before every load, how surge, bulkheads and baffles change your driving, and how much liquid you can load.

Liquids and gases move, sit high and weigh a lot. That is why tank vehicles need their own endorsement and knowledge test. This lesson follows Section 8 of the CDL manual; the test also draws on what you learn for General Knowledge, air brakes, combination vehicles and hazardous materials.

Who needs the tank endorsement

You need the N (tank vehicle) endorsement when you drive a Class A or Class B vehicle and haul a liquid or liquefied gas in tanks that are permanently or temporarily attached to the vehicle or chassis, where each tank is rated for more than 119 gallons and all tanks together for 1,000 gallons or more. The liquid does not have to be hazardous: milk counts. In Class C, the endorsement is needed only when the tank carries hazardous materials in liquid or gas form.

Worked example: do you need the N endorsement? The rule has two tank-size conditions, and both must be true. Picture a Class A vehicle with one tank rated for more than 119 gallons, but all its tanks together rated for less than 1,000 gallons. The second condition is not met, so the size rule alone does not require the endorsement. Now picture the same vehicle with total capacity of 1,000 gallons or more, where every individual tank is rated at 119 gallons or less. This time the first condition fails. The endorsement is needed only when each tank is over 119 gallons and the total reaches 1,000 gallons or more, or when a Class C vehicle hauls hazardous liquids or gases in tanks of that size. Check your state’s rules too, and when in doubt, ask your licensing agency.

Inspecting a tank vehicle

Tank vehicles come in many types, so learn your vehicle’s operator manual. Inspect before loading, unloading or driving.

Leaks come first

The most important thing to look for is a leak. Hauling liquid or gas in a leaking tank is a crime: you will be cited, stopped from driving on, and you may have to pay for the clean-up. Check:

  • the tank body (shell) for dents and leaks;
  • the intake, discharge and cut-off valves, and that each is in the right position before you load, unload or move;
  • pipes, connections and hoses, especially at the joints;
  • manhole covers (with gaskets, closing properly) and vents (kept clear).

Special equipment

If your tanker has them, make sure these work: emergency shut-off systems, a built-in fire extinguisher, grounding and bonding cables, and vapor recovery kits. Know which emergency equipment your vehicle must carry and check it is there and working. Never drive with a valve or a manhole cover open.

A short checklist before you move

Put the inspection in an order you can repeat every time, so nothing gets skipped when you are in a hurry:

  1. Walk around and look under the vehicle for any sign of a leak, then check the shell for dents.
  2. Check every valve (intake, discharge and cut-off) and confirm it sits in the right position for what you are about to do: load, unload or drive.
  3. Follow the pipes, connections and hoses, paying most attention to the joints.
  4. Close and inspect the manhole covers and make sure the vents are clear.
  5. Test the special equipment your tanker has: shut-off system, extinguisher, grounding and bonding cables, vapor recovery kit.
  6. Confirm the required emergency equipment is on board and works.

A leak at any step ends the trip. The manual treats hauling in a leaking tank as a crime, not just a mistake.

Why tankers handle differently

High center of gravity

Much of the load’s weight sits high off the road, so a tanker is top-heavy and rolls over easily. Tests have shown tankers can overturn at the posted speed of a curve. Take curves and on-ramps and off-ramps well below the posted speed.

Liquid surge

In a partly filled tank the liquid sloshes. When you stop, a wave runs forward and back; when it hits the end of the tank, it pushes the truck the way the wave is moving. On ice, that push can move a truck that has already stopped forward into an intersection. Know how your loaded vehicle behaves before you rely on it.

Bulkheads, baffles and smooth bore tanks

Tank design What it does What you must do
Bulkheads Walls that split one tank into several smaller tanks. When loading and unloading the compartments, watch how the weight is spread, so neither the front nor the rear carries too much.
Baffles Bulkheads with holes that let liquid flow through slowly. They calm front-to-back surge, but side-to-side surge still happens and can roll the vehicle.
Un-baffled (“smooth bore”) Nothing inside slows the liquid, so front-to-back surge is very strong. Usually used for food such as milk, because sanitation rules forbid baffles that are hard to clean. Drive slowly and carefully, above all when starting and stopping.

Outage: never fill a tank completely

Liquids expand as they warm up, so a cargo tank must never be loaded totally full. The empty space left for expansion is called outage. Different liquids expand differently and need different amounts, so know the outage requirement of whatever you haul.

How much to load

A full tank of a dense liquid, such as some acids, can be over the legal weight limits, so heavy liquids often go in partly filled tanks. How much you load depends on three things: how much the liquid will expand on the trip, how much it weighs, and the legal weight limits.

Safe driving rules for tankers

  • Drive smoothly. Start, slow down and stop gently; make turns and lane changes smoothly too.
  • Control surge. Keep steady pressure on the brake and do not let off too early as you stop. Brake well before the stop and leave a longer following distance.
  • Emergency stops: use controlled or stab braking, and do not steer sharply while braking: the tanker can roll over.
  • Curves: slow down before the curve, then accelerate slightly through it. The posted speed may be too fast for a tank vehicle.
  • Stopping distance: a wet road doubles your normal stopping distance, and an empty tanker may take longer to stop than a full one.
  • Skids: do not over-steer, over-accelerate or over-brake. If the drive wheels or trailer wheels of a tank trailer start to skid, it can jackknife; act at once to get traction back.

What goes wrong, and why

Problem What causes it What you do about it
Rollover A high center of gravity: much of the weight rides high off the road. Side-to-side surge can add to it, even with baffles. Take curves and ramps well under the posted speed, and never steer sharply while braking.
Surge push Liquid in a partly filled tank moves back and forth, and the wave shoves the truck when it hits the end of the tank. Brake early, keep steady pressure and do not let go too soon. Leave extra following distance.
Skid and jackknife Over-steering, over-accelerating or over-braking, which lets the drive wheels or trailer wheels slide. Be smooth, and act at once to get traction back if a skid starts.
Overfilled tank Liquids expand as they warm and there was no room left for them. Leave outage that fits the liquid you are hauling.
Overweight load A dense liquid, such as some acids, can make a full tank heavier than the legal weight limits allow. Load less than a full tank of heavy liquids, using expansion, weight and the legal limits to decide.
Leak Damage or poor fit at the shell, valves, pipes, hoses, manhole covers or vents. Find it in the inspection and do not move the load until it is fixed.

Easy to mix up

Looks alike How they differ How to tell them apart
Bulkheads and baffles A bulkhead is a wall that divides a tank into smaller tanks. A baffle is a bulkhead with holes, so the liquid can still flow through slowly. Solid wall means separate compartments. Wall with holes means one tank with calmer surge.
Baffled and smooth bore tanks Baffles slow front-to-back surge. A smooth bore tank has nothing inside, so that surge is very strong. Smooth bore is the one to drive most gently, above all when starting and stopping.
Front-to-back and side-to-side surge Baffles help control the first kind only. The second kind can still happen in a baffled tank and can roll the vehicle. A baffled tank is calmer, not safe to throw into a curve.
Outage and load limit Outage is the empty room left for expansion. The load limit also depends on the weight of the liquid and the legal weight limits. Outage is about volume as the liquid warms. The weight limit is about how heavy the liquid is.
Empty and full tanker You might expect an empty tanker to stop faster, but the manual says it may take longer to stop than a full one. Do not assume that less weight means a shorter stop.
Wet road and normal stopping distance Wet roads double the normal distance you need. Plan for twice the space, then add more for a tanker.

Check yourself

Cover the answers with your hand and try each question aloud.

  1. How is a bulkhead different from a baffle? A bulkhead splits the tank into separate smaller tanks. A baffle is a bulkhead with holes that lets liquid pass.
  2. Should you take a curve or ramp at the posted speed? No. Go well below it, because tankers can roll over at the posted speed.
  3. What makes a smooth bore tank harder to drive? Nothing inside slows the liquid, so front-to-back surge is very strong.
  4. What three things decide how much you load? How much the liquid expands, how much it weighs and the legal weight limits.
  5. What is outage? The space left in a tank so the liquid has room to expand.
  6. How do you help control surge? Brake early, keep steady pressure on the brake and do not release too soon.
  7. Which two features of a tanker call for extra care? The high center of gravity and the movement of the liquid.

Remember for the test

  • N endorsement: tanks over 119 gallons each and 1,000 gallons or more in total, in Class A or B (Class C only for hazardous liquids or gases).
  • Leaks are the top inspection item; never drive with open valves or manhole covers.
  • Baffles reduce front-to-back surge only; smooth bore tanks have the strongest surge.
  • Outage = room left for the liquid to expand.
  • Brake early with steady pressure; take curves well below the posted speed.
