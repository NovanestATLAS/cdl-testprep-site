CDL General Knowledge: Seeing, Signaling, Speed and Space Management

How a professional driver sees, signals and manages speed and space: the 12 to 15 second look-ahead, mirror checks, where to put warning triangles, the four parts of stopping distance, slippery roads, following distance and wide turns.

Not looking properly is a major cause of crashes, and speed is a major cause of the deadly ones. This second General Knowledge lesson covers Sections 2.4 to 2.7 of the CDL manual: seeing, communicating, controlling speed and managing the space around a big vehicle.

Seeing ahead

Many drivers look ahead, but not far enough. Stopping or changing lanes in a heavy vehicle takes a lot of room, so you need to know early what traffic is doing.

  • Look 12 to 15 seconds ahead, the distance you will cover in that time: about one block at low speed and about a quarter of a mile at highway speed. Otherwise you end up braking hard or changing lanes at the last moment.
  • Looking far ahead does not mean ignoring what is close: good drivers keep shifting their attention near and far.
  • Watch for vehicles entering the road, moving into your lane or turning, and for brake lights. A light that has been green for a long time will probably change before you reach it, so start slowing and be ready to stop.

Mirrors: seeing to the sides and rear

Check mirror adjustment before every trip, with the trailers straight, so each mirror shows a bit of your own vehicle as a reference point. Then check them regularly to know where other vehicles are and whether they slip into your blind spots, which no mirror shows. Mirrors also let you watch your tires (one way to spot a tire fire) and open cargo: loose straps, ropes or chains, or a tarp that flaps or balloons.

Some moments need more than regular checks:

  • Lane changes: check before you change to make sure there is room, after signaling to be sure your blind spot is still empty, again just as you begin to move over, and once you have finished.
  • Turns: make sure the rear of the vehicle will clear everything.
  • Merges: make sure the gap is big enough.
  • Tight maneuvers: check often for clearance.

Glance at the mirrors quickly and go back to the road ahead; staring at them means traveling a long way blind. Convex mirrors (“fisheye”, “spot”, “bug-eye”) show a wider view, but everything in them looks smaller and farther away than it really is.

Communicating

Other drivers cannot know what you will do until you tell them.

Signals

  • Turns: signal early (the best way to stop others from trying to pass you), keep the signal on through the whole turn with both hands on the wheel, and cancel it afterward if it does not cancel itself.
  • Lane changes: signal first, then move over slowly and smoothly so a driver you missed has time to honk or get out of the way.
  • Slowing down: tap the brake pedal lightly to flash your brake lights when there is trouble ahead that drivers behind you cannot see, before a tight turn (brake early and slow gradually), and before stopping on the road to unload or at a railroad crossing. When driving very slowly, use the four-way flashers if your state allows it.
  • Never direct traffic by waving others past: if they crash, you could be blamed and pay many thousands of dollars.

Making yourself seen and heard

  • When passing a car, a person on foot or a cyclist, assume they do not see you. Tap the horn lightly where legal, or at night briefly switch from low beams to high beams and back, and drive so you avoid a crash even if they never notice you.
  • At dawn and dusk, and in rain or snow, turn on your headlights (low beams), not only the clearance lights. If you have trouble seeing others, they have trouble seeing you.
  • Use the horn when needed, but not without reason: it can startle people and cause danger.

Stopped at the roadside: warning devices

Turn on the four-way flashers whenever you pull off and stop, especially at night; drivers have hit parked trucks thinking they were still moving. If you stop on a road or its shoulder, put out your warning devices within ten minutes, holding them between you and the oncoming traffic as you walk.

  • One-way or divided highway: 10 feet, 100 feet and 200 feet behind the vehicle, toward approaching traffic.
  • Two-lane road with traffic both ways, or an undivided highway: one within 10 feet of the front or rear corner, plus one 100 feet behind it and one 100 feet in front of it, placed on the shoulder or in your lane.
  • Hill, curve or other obstruction: if drivers cannot see the vehicle within 500 feet, move the rear device farther back so they get a warning.

Controlling speed

Adjust speed to traction, curves, visibility, traffic and hills.

Stopping distance

Total stopping distance is the sum of three parts: the distance covered while you perceive the hazard, while you react, and while the brakes work. At 55 mph in ideal conditions:

  • Perception: an alert driver needs about 1¾ seconds to recognize a hazard, about 142 feet.
  • Reaction: ¾ second to 1 second to get a foot on the brake, about 61 feet.
  • Braking: about 216 feet on dry pavement with good brakes.
  • Total: at least 419 feet.

Speed raises both braking distance and impact force with the square of the speed: double from 20 to 40 mph and both are 4 times greater; triple to 60 mph and they are 9 times greater; at 80 mph they are 16 times what they were at 20 mph. At 60 mph you need more than a football field to stop.

Weight matters too. A heavy vehicle makes the brakes work harder and absorb more heat, yet trucks are designed to brake best when loaded: an empty truck needs a longer stopping distance because it has less traction.

Matching speed to the road

Traction is the grip between the tires and the road; without it you cannot steer or brake. A wet road can double your stopping distance.

  • Wet road: slow down by about one-third (from 55 to about 35 mph).
  • Packed snow: slow down by half or more.
  • Ice: slow to a crawl and get off the road as soon as it is safe.

Slippery spots are not always obvious. Shaded areas stay icy after open road has melted; bridges freeze before the road, especially near 32 degrees Fahrenheit; slightly melting ice is wetter and much slicker; black ice is so thin and clear that the road just looks wet, so be wary whenever it is below freezing and the pavement looks wet. Feel the front of a mirror, its support or the antenna: ice there means the road is probably icing up too. The first minutes of rain are the worst, as water mixes with oil on the road until more rain washes it off.

Hydroplaning happens when the tires ride on water or slush and lose contact with the road, so you may be unable to steer or brake. It can happen at speeds as low as 30 mph when there is a lot of water, and it is more likely with low tire pressure or worn tread. Do not brake: let off the accelerator and push in the clutch so the wheels turn freely and the vehicle slows. Signs of standing water: raindrops hitting the pavement, spray thrown up by tires, and clear reflections on the road.

Curves, sight distance, traffic and downgrades

  • Curves: too fast and the tires either lose grip and slide off the road, or keep grip and the vehicle rolls over. Tests show a truck with a high center of gravity may tip over even at the speed posted for the curve. Slow down before the curve (braking in it can lock the wheels), never go over the posted curve speed, and be in a gear that lets you accelerate slightly through it.
  • Sight distance: drive at a speed that lets you stop inside the stretch of road you can see. Slow down in fog or rain, and whenever you must drive on low beams at night.
  • Traffic flow: when traffic is heavy, matching its speed is safest, as long as that speed is legal and safe. Truck speed limits are lower than car limits in many states, by as much as 15 mph, so take care passing and changing lanes. Speeding saves little time: you keep passing, which raises the chance of a crash and tires you out.
  • Downgrades: choose a speed that suits the total weight, the length and steepness of the grade, the road and the weather. Never exceed a posted limit or “Maximum Safe Speed” sign, and use engine braking as your main way to control speed: it is strongest in a low gear near the governed rpm. Save the brakes for slowing or stopping when traffic requires it.
  • Work zones: more people are hurt and killed in them by speeding than by anything else. Keep to the posted limit, watch your speedometer on long stretches, and slow down more in bad weather or when workers are close to the road.

Managing space

Space gives you time to think and act when something goes wrong, and big vehicles need more of it to stop and turn.

Space ahead

The vehicle trucks and buses hit most often is the one in front, and the usual cause is following too closely. A smaller vehicle ahead can probably stop faster than you.

The rule: below 40 mph, leave at least one second per 10 feet of your vehicle’s length; above 40 mph, add 1 second. A 40-foot vehicle needs 4 seconds (5 seconds over 40 mph); a 60-foot rig needs 6 seconds (7 seconds over 40 mph). To measure it, wait for the vehicle ahead to pass a shadow, a pavement marking or another landmark, then count “one thousand-and-one, one thousand-and-two” until you reach the same spot. If your count is short, drop back and count again. On a slippery road you need much more room.

Space behind

You cannot stop people from tailgating, but you can make it safer. If a heavy load slows you, keep to the right lane, and going uphill, pass a slow vehicle only if you can get around it quickly and safely. You are most likely to be tailgated when going slowly and in bad weather. Then:

  • Avoid quick changes: signal early and slow down very gradually.
  • Leave more room ahead, so you will not need sudden moves and the tailgater can get around you more easily.
  • Do not speed up: being tailgated at low speed is safer.
  • No tricks such as turning on your taillights or flashing the brake lights.

Space to the sides, overhead and below

  • Sides: keep the vehicle centered in its lane and avoid driving alongside others, who may turn into you or box you in. If you must, leave as much room as you can and drop back or pull ahead so the other driver can see you. Strong winds make lane-keeping hard, especially for lighter vehicles and right out of tunnels.
  • Overhead: do not trust posted clearances at bridges and overpasses, since new paving or packed snow may have reduced them. An empty van rides higher than a loaded one, so clearing a bridge loaded does not mean you will clear it empty. If in doubt, go slowly or take another route. On roads that tilt, keep a little toward the center to clear signs and trees at the edge, and before backing in, get out and check for branches and wires.
  • Below: a heavy load can leave very little ground clearance. Dirt roads, unpaved yards, drainage channels and railroad tracks can hang up a vehicle or a low trailer, so cross them carefully.

Space for turns

Wide turns and off-tracking (the rear wheels following a shorter path than the front) let big vehicles hit things in turns.

  • Right turns: turn slowly. If you cannot turn without swinging into another lane, swing wide as you finish the turn and keep the rear close to the curb, so no one can pass you on the right. Do not swing left as you start: the driver behind may read it as a left turn and try to slip past you on the right. If you must cross into the oncoming lane, give oncoming traffic room to pass or stop, but never back up for them.
  • Left turns: wait until you are in the middle of the intersection before turning, or off-tracking may swing your left side into another vehicle. With two left-turn lanes, use the right-hand one.

Space to cross or enter traffic

Heavy vehicles accelerate slowly, so you need a much bigger gap than a car, and more still with a heavy load. Before crossing a road, make sure you can clear it completely before traffic arrives.

Remember for the test

  • Look 12 to 15 seconds ahead.
  • Warning devices within ten minutes: 10, 100 and 200 feet on a divided highway; 10 feet and 100 feet each way on a two-lane road; farther back if drivers cannot see you within 500 feet.
  • Stopping at 55 mph: 142 + 61 + 216 = at least 419 feet.
  • Wet road: one-third slower. Packed snow: half or more. Ice: crawl, then stop driving.
  • Following distance: one second per 10 feet of length, plus 1 second over 40 mph.
  • Empty trucks need more distance to stop. Engine braking is the main tool on downgrades.
