CDL General Knowledge: Night, Fog, Winter, Heat, Railroad Crossings and Mountains

Driving when conditions turn against you: fatigue and glare at night, fog, winter and summer checks, wet brakes, how to approach and cross railroad tracks, and how to get a heavy vehicle down a mountain without losing the brakes.

Darkness, fog, snow, heat, train tracks and long grades all shrink your margin for error. This fourth General Knowledge lesson covers Sections 2.11 to 2.16 of the CDL manual.

Driving at night

Night is more dangerous because hazards appear later, leaving less time to react. The problems come from three places: the driver, the road and the vehicle.

The driver: vision, glare and fatigue

  • Vision. Have your eyes checked regularly; you may not know they have weakened until they are tested. If your license requires corrective lenses, wear them every time you drive, however short the trip (driving without them is illegal), keep a spare pair in the vehicle, and never wear tinted lenses or sunglasses at night: they cut the light you need.
  • Glare. Bright lights can blind you for several seconds. Even two seconds is dangerous: at 55 mph you cover more than half a football field.
  • Fatigue is physical or mental tiredness from strain, repetitive work, illness or lack of sleep. Like alcohol and drugs, it impairs vision and judgment, leads to errors of speed and distance, slows your reaction to hazards and can put you to sleep at the wheel.

Drowsy driving is a leading cause of crashes: NHTSA estimates 100,000 police-reported crashes a year come from it. In one poll, 60% of Americans said they had driven while sleepy and 36 percent (103 million people) admitted actually falling asleep at the wheel. At least 15% of heavy truck crashes involve fatigue, and a study of 80 long-haul drivers found they averaged less than 5 hours of sleep a day.

Who is most at risk: young men, shift workers, commercial drivers (especially long-haul), and anyone with an untreated sleep disorder or short of sleep. Sleepiness peaks at night, especially after midnight, and in mid-afternoon; many heavy vehicle crashes happen between midnight and 6 a.m. Your risk also rises if you slept 6 hours or less (it triples), take sedating medicines such as cold tablets or antihistamines, work more than 60 hours a week (40% more risk), hold a second job on top of shift work, drive alone on long, dark or monotonous roads, or have just flown across time zones.

Warning signs: trouble focusing, heavy eyelids or frequent blinking, repeated yawning or eye rubbing, wandering thoughts, not remembering the last few miles, missed exits or signs, trouble holding your head up, drifting out of the lane, tailgating, hitting a rumble strip, and feeling restless or irritable. Pushing on when tired is a major cause of fatal crashes. Stop and sleep for the night, or take a 15 to 20 minute nap.

Before a trip, get enough sleep (adults need 8 to 9 hours), plan the route and stops, schedule driving for the hours you are normally awake, ride with a passenger if you can, avoid medicines that make you drowsy, see a doctor about daytime sleepiness, and exercise. On the road, wear sunglasses against glare in daylight, keep the cab cool, avoid heavy meals, take a break about every 100 miles or 2 hours on long trips, and share the driving. Caffeine helps only for a few hours and then wears off, and drugs may keep you awake but will not make you alert. The only real cure for drowsiness is sleep.

The road

Many roads are poorly lit, so you rely on your headlights. Pedestrians, joggers, cyclists and animals are hard to see, and at the other extreme, signals and hazards can be lost among signs, shop windows and other lights. Slow down when lighting is poor or confusing, and always keep your speed low enough to stop within the distance you can see. Be especially alert for drunk or drugged drivers around bar closing times.

The vehicle

  • Headlights: low beams light about 250 feet ahead and high beams about 350 to 500 feet, so match your speed to that distance. Dirty headlights can give only half their normal light, and badly aimed ones light the wrong place and blind others: have them adjusted by a qualified person.
  • Other lights: reflectors, marker, clearance, identification and taillights, turn signals and brake lights must all be clean and working; at night, signals are how other drivers learn what you will do.
  • Windshield and mirrors: bright lights make dirt on the glass glare and block your view, so clean them inside and out.

Night driving procedures

Be rested before you start, keep your glasses clean and unscratched, and do a complete inspection with extra attention to cleaning the lights and reflectors you can reach.

  • Switch to low beams when an oncoming vehicle is 500 feet away or closer, and when you are less than 500 feet behind another vehicle.
  • Do not look straight at oncoming headlights: look slightly to the right, at a lane line or the road edge. If others keep their high beams on, never answer with yours.
  • Use high beams whenever it is safe and legal, since low beams alone seriously limit how far you see.
  • Keep the cab dark: interior light off and instrument lights turned down.
  • Feeling sleepy? Pull off at the first safe spot. If a look in the mirror shows a sleepy face, or you simply feel sleepy, you are in danger: sleep.

Fog

Fog can arrive suddenly and thicken fast, and it may not thin out once you are in it. The best advice is not to drive in it: wait in a rest area or truck stop until it lifts. If you must drive:

  • Obey fog warning signs and slow down before entering fog.
  • Use low beams and fog lights, even in daylight, and turn on your four-way flashers so drivers behind see you sooner.
  • Lights ahead may belong to a vehicle that has left the road, so do not follow them blindly; use the roadside reflectors to judge curves.
  • Listen for traffic you cannot see, avoid passing, and stop at the roadside only if you absolutely must.

Winter driving

Winter checks

Do your normal inspection with extra attention to:

  • Coolant and antifreeze: enough to protect against freezing (a coolant tester checks this).
  • Defrosters and heater working, plus any mirror, battery box or fuel tank heaters you rely on.
  • Wipers and washers: blades in good shape and pressing firmly enough to clear snow, washer fluid with antifreeze in the reservoir. If you cannot see well enough, stop safely and fix it.
  • Tires: drive tires need tread to push through snow and steering tires to steer. The minimum is 4/32 inch in every major groove on the front tires and 2/32 inch on the others, and more is better; measure with a gauge.
  • Chains: carry the right number plus extra cross-links, check that they fit and are not damaged (broken hooks, worn cross-links, bent side chains), and practice fitting them before you need to.
  • Lights, windows and mirrors clean and clear of ice and snow before you start, and handholds, steps and deck plates clear so you do not slip.
  • Radiator shutters and winterfront: clear ice from the shutters and do not close the winterfront too far, or the engine may overheat and stop.
  • Exhaust: with the windows closed, a leak can let carbon monoxide into the cab. It makes you sleepy and can kill, so check for loose parts and signs of leaks.

Winter driving

Drive slowly and smoothly on slippery roads, and if it is very slippery, stop at the first safe place. Start off gently to get the feel of the road. Ice often forms first on bridges and overpasses; if the spray from other vehicles stops, ice has probably formed, and ice on your mirrors or wipers means the road is likely icy too.

  • Turn gently and brake no harder than you must. Do not use the engine brake or retarder: they can make the drive wheels skid.
  • Pass only if you have to, look far ahead to hold a steady speed, take curves slowly and never brake in them. Melting ice is even more slippery, so slow down more as temperatures rise toward thawing.
  • Stay out of the space beside other vehicles, leave a longer following distance, slow early for traffic jams, and give snowplows and salt and sand trucks plenty of room.

Wet brakes

Heavy rain and deep water make brakes weak, uneven or grabby, which can mean poor stopping, locked wheels, pulling to one side, or a jackknife with a trailer. Avoid deep puddles and flowing water when you can. If you must go through:

  1. Slow down and select a low gear.
  2. Brake gently, so the linings press on the drums or discs and keep water, mud and sand out.
  3. Raise the engine rpm and cross while keeping light pressure on the brakes.
  4. Once out, keep light brake pressure for a short distance to heat and dry the brakes.
  5. When it is safe and no one is behind you, make a test stop; if the brakes are not right, dry them more. Do not use heavy brake and accelerator together, which overheats drums and linings.

Very hot weather

  • Tires: check mounting and pressure, and inspect them every two hours or 100 miles. Pressure rises with heat, but never let air out, or it will be too low once the tires cool. If a tire is too hot to touch, stay stopped until it cools, or it may blow out or burn.
  • Engine oil cools the engine as well as lubricating it: keep the level and the oil temperature in range.
  • Coolant: start with enough water and antifreeze (antifreeze helps in heat too) and watch the temperature gauge. Sight glasses and recovery tanks let you check the level hot, and a tank outside the pressurized system can be topped up hot. Never open the radiator cap, or anything else on the pressurized side, while the system is still hot, since steam and boiling water can spray out under pressure; a cap you can hold in your bare hand is probably cool enough to open. Then: engine off, wait for it to cool, protect your hands, turn the cap slowly to the first stop to release the pressure seal, step back, then press down and turn it off, add coolant if needed, and close the cap fully.
  • Belts and hoses: a loose belt will not drive the water pump or fan and the engine overheats; a hose that bursts can lead to engine failure or even fire.
  • On the road: watch for bleeding tar, which is very slippery, and drive slowly enough that the tires and engine do not overheat, especially in the desert.

Railroad crossings

Expect a train at every crossing: it is very hard to judge how far away a train is and how fast it is moving.

Kinds of crossings and warnings

  • Passive crossings have no traffic control device: you alone recognize the crossing, look for trains and decide whether there is room to cross.
  • Active crossings have flashing red lights, with or without bells, or lights, bells and gates.
  • The round black-on-yellow advance warning sign means slow down, look and listen, and be ready to stop if a train is coming. Passenger and hazmat vehicles must stop. Pavement markings (an X with RR and a no-passing line on two-lane roads) mean the same.
  • The crossbuck marks the crossing and means yield to trains; a sign below it shows the number of tracks when there is more than one. Where there is no white stop line, vehicles that must stop do so no closer than 15 feet and no farther than 50 feet from the nearest rail. A school bus must keep its front behind a stop line where there is one.
  • Flashing red lights: stop, a train is coming, and make sure every track is clear before crossing. Gates: stop when the lights start, before the gate comes down, and stay stopped until the gates rise and the lights stop flashing.

At the crossing

  • Never race a train. Slow down enough to see trains coming from any direction and to stop short of the tracks.
  • Do not count on hearing a horn (some crossings are quiet zones, and cab noise can hide it) or on signals, gates and flaggers alone.
  • A train on one track can hide another: look both ways again after a train passes. City crossings and rail yards are as dangerous as rural ones.
  • When a stop is required by your cargo or by law, check traffic behind you, slow gradually, use a pullout lane if there is one, and turn on the four-way flashers.
  • Never let traffic trap you on the tracks: only start across when you can clear them completely. Clearing one track takes a typical tractor-trailer 14 seconds or more; clearing two takes over 15 seconds. Do not shift gears on the tracks.
  • Steep approaches can hang up low-slung trailers (lowboys, car carriers, moving vans, possum-belly livestock trailers) and a single-axle tractor pulling a long trailer whose landing gear is set for a tandem-axle tractor.
  • If you get stuck, get out and away from the tracks, look for the emergency information on the signal housing or a signpost, call 911 or the emergency number, and describe the location with landmarks and the DOT number if posted.

Mountain driving

Gravity slows you on the way up and speeds you up on the way down. The steeper or longer the grade and the heavier the load, the lower the gears you need. Find out about long, steep grades on your route before you set out.

Going down, you must be slow enough for the brakes to hold you without overheating. Hot brakes fade: you have to press harder and harder for the same effect, until they may not stop you at all. Brakes out of adjustment stop doing their share first, leaving the rest to overheat, and hot linings wear faster, so check adjustment often.

  • Safe speed: pick it for the total weight, the length and steepness of the grade, the road and the weather, never above a posted limit or “Maximum Safe Speed”, and heed the grade warning signs.
  • Gear: shift down before the grade begins. Once speed has built up you will not get into a lower gear and may lose engine braking altogether; forcing an automatic down at speed can damage it. The old rule was to go down in the gear you would climb in, but modern trucks are slippery and powerful, so they often need a lower gear going down than going up.
  • Engine braking does the main work, strongest in a low gear near the governed rpm. The service brakes only supplement it.

The braking method on a downgrade

Once in the right low gear:

  1. Brake just hard enough to feel a definite slowdown.
  2. Release when you are about five mph below your safe speed; each application should last about three seconds.
  3. When the speed climbs back to your safe speed, repeat.

With a safe speed of 40 mph, you would wait until you reach 40 mph, brake down to 35 mph, release, and repeat to the bottom of the grade.

Escape ramps stop runaway vehicles with a long bed of loose, soft material, sometimes with an upgrade. Signs mark them; know where they are on your route.

Easy to mix up

Looks alike How they differ How to tell them apart
500 feet and 250 feet at night 500 feet is when you switch to low beams: when an oncoming vehicle is that close, or when you are that close behind another vehicle. 250 feet is about how far low beams light the road, while high beams reach about 350 to 500 feet. One number is a trigger for dimming. The other is how far you can see.
The 100 miles or 2 hours break and the 100 miles or two hours tire check On a long trip, take a break about every 100 miles or 2 hours to fight drowsiness. In very hot weather, inspect the tires every two hours or 100 miles. Same numbers, different job: one rests you, the other checks your tires.
Nap and sleep A 15 to 20 minute nap can help in the short term, or you can stop and sleep for the night. Caffeine wears off in a few hours and drugs may keep you awake but will not make you alert. Only sleep cures drowsiness. A nap is the short version.
Passive and active crossings A passive crossing has no traffic control device, so you decide whether it is safe. An active crossing has flashing red lights, with or without bells, or lights, bells and gates. Passive means nothing helps you. Active means something warns you, but never count on it alone.
The advance warning sign and the crossbuck The round black-on-yellow sign comes first: slow down, look and listen, and be ready to stop. Passenger and hazmat vehicles must stop. The crossbuck marks the crossing itself and means yield to trains. The round sign says get ready. The crossbuck says you have arrived.
15 to 50 feet and 14 seconds at a crossing 15 to 50 feet is where you stop, measured from the nearest rail. 14 seconds or more is how long a typical tractor-trailer takes to clear one track. The first is a distance when you stop. The second is time when you go.
Engine braking on a grade and the retarder on ice On a long downgrade, engine braking does the main work and the service brakes only supplement it. On slippery roads, do not use the engine brake or retarder, because the drive wheels may skid. Good traction: use the engine. Slippery road: leave it off.
Wet brakes and brake fade Wet brakes are weak, uneven or grabby after heavy rain or deep water, and you dry them with light pressure. Fade is overheated brakes that need ever more pressure on a long grade. Water gives you grabby or weak brakes. Heat gives you fade.
A hot radiator cap and a cool one Never open the cap while the system is hot, because steam and boiling water can spray out. A cap you can hold in your bare hand is probably cool enough to open. If you cannot hold it, wait.
The old gear rule and the modern rule The old rule was to go down in the gear you would climb in. Modern trucks are slippery and powerful, so they often need a lower gear going down than going up. Do not assume the climbing gear is enough. Shift down before the grade begins.

Remember for the test

  • Low beams for oncoming traffic within 500 feet and when following within 500 feet. Low beams show about 250 feet, high beams 350 to 500 feet.
  • Drowsy? The only cure is sleep; a 15 to 20 minute nap can help. Breaks about every 100 miles or 2 hours.
  • Fog: slow down before it, low beams and fog lights, four-way flashers, no passing.
  • Winter: no engine brake or retarder on slippery roads; bridges freeze first.
  • Hot weather: check tires every two hours or 100 miles; never let air out of hot tires.
  • Railroad crossings: stop 15 to 50 feet from the nearest rail; never shift on the tracks; at least 14 seconds to clear one track.
  • Downgrades: low gear before the top, brake down to five mph under the safe speed, about three seconds per application.
