CDL Passenger Endorsement Study Guide: Driving a Bus Safely

Everything the passenger endorsement test covers: inspecting a bus inside and out, what riders may and may not bring aboard, safe stops and curves, crossings, and the practices that are never allowed.

The P (passenger) endorsement is needed to drive a vehicle designed to carry 16 or more people, counting the driver. To get it you study Sections 2 and 4 of the CDL manual and pass their knowledge test (adding the Section 5 air brakes test if the bus has air brakes) and the skills tests in a bus of your license class. This lesson follows Section 4.

Before you drive: inspecting the bus

Start by reading the previous driver’s inspection report. Sign it only if the defects it lists have been certified as repaired or as not needing repair: your signature says so.

Systems that must work

Service brakes (and air hose couplings if the bus pulls a trailer), parking brake, steering, lights and reflectors, tires, horn, wipers, mirrors, any coupling devices, wheels and rims, and emergency equipment. The front wheels may not have recapped or re-grooved tires.

Emergency equipment means a fire extinguisher, spare electrical fuses (unless the bus uses circuit breakers), and warning devices: three reflective triangles, or at least six fusees, or three liquid burning flares.

Outside and inside

  • As you walk around, close any open emergency exits and any open access panels (baggage, restroom service, engine).
  • Check the interior every time: unattended buses get damaged. Aisles and stairwells must be clear, and handholds, railings, floor covering, signaling devices (including a restroom emergency buzzer), emergency exit handles and seats must be in safe condition, with every seat firmly fastened.
  • Never drive with an emergency door or window open. The “Emergency Exit” sign must be clearly visible, and a red emergency door light, if fitted, must work and be on at night or whenever your outside lights are on.
  • Some roof hatches can be locked partly open for air, but not as a habit, and remember the bus is taller with them open.
  • Wear your seat belt.

Loading and starting the trip

Keep carry-on bags out of doorways and aisles. Stow baggage and freight so you can move freely, riders can get out through any door or window in an emergency, and falling or shifting bags cannot hurt anyone.

Hazardous materials on a bus

Most hazardous materials may not travel on a bus. Shippers have to show on each such container what the material is called, its identification number and its hazard label. Watch for the four-inch, diamond-shaped labels.

They cover nine hazard classes, from explosives (Class 1) through gases, flammable liquids and solids, oxidizers, poisons, radioactive materials and corrosives to miscellaneous hazardous materials (Class 9). Familiar examples include propane, gasoline and battery acid. The HazMat lesson explains the classes in detail.

Allowed on a bus: drugs, emergency hospital supplies, small-arms ammunition with an ORM-D label, and small amounts of some other hazardous materials when the shipper has no other way to send them. A bus must never carry:

  • Tear gas, irritating material, poison gas of Division 2.3, or Class 6 poison in liquid form.
  • Over 100 pounds of solid Class 6 poisons.
  • Explosives where people ride, except small-arms ammunition.
  • Labeled radioactive materials where people ride.
  • Over 500 pounds in total of the allowed hazardous materials, or over 100 pounds of any single class.

Riders may try to bring unlabeled hazards on board: do not let them carry common ones such as car batteries or gasoline.

The standee line

Nobody may stand in front of the back of the driver’s seat. A bus built for standing riders has a two-inch line on the floor (or another marker) called the standee line, and standing riders must stay behind it.

At stops and at the destination

Announce the location, why you are stopping, the next departure time and the bus number. Remind riders to take their carry-ons, and warn them of any step down to the aisle before you come to a full stop. Charter drivers should keep the bus closed to riders until departure time, which helps prevent theft and vandalism.

On the road

  • Explain the rules early. Many carriers have rules on smoking, drinking or playing radios; mention them at the start to avoid trouble later. While driving, keep scanning the inside of the bus as well as the road.
  • Smooth starts and stops. Riders fall when the bus moves while they are getting on or off. Tell them to watch their step and wait until they are seated or holding on before you move off.
  • Disruptive or drunk riders still have to be kept safe: do not put them off somewhere dangerous. The next scheduled stop or a well-lit place with other people around may be safer.
  • Where bus crashes happen. Intersections are a common place, even when other traffic has a stop sign or signal. Buses pulling away from stops scrape mirrors or hit passing cars: watch for poles and branches, and wait until there is a gap big enough to accelerate into. Never assume others will brake for you.
  • Curves. The posted speed on a banked curve is meant for cars in good weather and can be too fast for a bus, which may roll over on a dry road or slide off on a slippery one. If the bus leans toward the outside of the curve, you are going too fast.

Railroad crossings and drawbridges

  • Stop between 15 and 50 feet before a railroad crossing, look and listen both ways, and open the front door if it helps you see or hear. After a train passes, check for a second train on other tracks. With a manual transmission, never shift while crossing.
  • You may slow down and check carefully instead of stopping at streetcar crossings, where a police officer or flagger directs traffic, at a green signal, and at crossings marked “exempt” or “abandoned”.
  • At a drawbridge with no signal light or attendant, stop 50 feet or more ahead of the draw and make sure it is fully closed. With a green light, or an attendant who controls traffic when the bridge opens, slow down and make sure it is safe instead.

After the trip

Inspect the bus at the end of every shift. Drivers for interstate carriers must write a report for each bus driven, listing any defect that affects safety or could cause a breakdown, or stating that there are none. Report damage riders cause to handholds, seats, emergency exits and windows so it is fixed before the next trip. Mass transit drivers also check the passenger signaling devices and brake-door interlocks.

Prohibited practices

  • Refueling with riders on board, unless it cannot be avoided, and never in a closed building with riders aboard.
  • Chatting with riders or doing anything else that distracts you while driving.
  • Towing or pushing a disabled bus with riders inside, unless getting off would be unsafe; then move it only to the nearest safe place to let them off.

Brake-door interlocks

Some urban transit coaches have an interlock that applies the brakes and holds the throttle at idle while the rear door is open, and releases when the door closes. It is a safety device, not a parking brake: always set the parking brake when you park.

Remember for the test

  • 16 or more people including the driver: P endorsement.
  • No recapped or re-grooved tires on the front wheels.
  • Never on board: tear gas, irritating material, Division 2.3 poison gas, liquid Class 6 poison; nor over 100 pounds of solid Class 6, nor over 500 pounds of allowed hazardous materials in total.
  • Standing riders stay behind the standee line.
  • Railroad crossings: stop 15 to 50 feet before the tracks. Drawbridges without signal or attendant: stop 50 feet or more ahead of the draw.
