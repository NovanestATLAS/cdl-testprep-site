CDL General Knowledge: Alcohol and Drugs, HazMat Basics and Cargo Safety

This sixth and last General Knowledge lesson covers what you put in your body before you drive, the hazardous materials rules that apply to every commercial driver (not only HazMat drivers), and Section 3 of the CDL manual on hauling cargo safely.

Alcohol and driving

The CDL rules

  • It is illegal to drive a CMV with a blood alcohol concentration (BAC) of .04% or more, and by driving a CMV you are considered to have agreed to alcohol testing.
  • Any detectable alcohol below .04% puts you out of service for 24 hours.
  • A first offense for driving at .04% or more, driving under the influence of alcohol or a controlled substance, or refusing a test costs your CDL for at least one year; the same applies to leaving the scene of a crash involving a CMV, using a CMV in a felony, driving while your CDL is suspended, and causing a death through negligent driving of a CMV. If it happens while you drive a vehicle placarded for hazardous materials, the loss is at least three years.
  • A second offense means losing the CDL for life, and so does using a CMV in a felony involving controlled substances.

How alcohol works

Alcohol passes straight into the bloodstream and reaches the brain. A little leaves the body through breath, sweat and urine; the liver handles the rest, but only at a fixed rate of about one-third of an ounce an hour, which is less than one standard drink holds. Only time sobers you up: black coffee and cold showers do not. Drink faster than the liver can keep up and the alcohol builds in your blood.

A standard drink is the same amount of alcohol whatever its form: 12 ounces of beer at 5%, 5 ounces of wine at 12%, or a 1.5-ounce shot of 80 proof liquor. “Just a couple of beers” carries as much alcohol as two glasses of wine or two shots.

Your BAC depends on how much you drink, how fast you drink, and your weight (a smaller person reaches the same BAC on less). For some people, one drink is enough to cause impairment, and even at .08 coordination and judgment are clearly impaired.

What it does to a driver

Alcohol first reaches the parts of the brain in charge of judgment and self-control, which is why drinkers often do not realize they are getting drunk. As BAC climbs, it attacks muscle control, vision, depth perception, night vision, coordination and reaction time. The results on the road: straddling lanes or driving in the wrong lane, jerky starts, forgetting signals or lights, running stop signs and red lights, bad passing, driving too fast or too slow, weaving and hitting curbs. Drinking drivers are involved in crashes that kill more than 20,000 people a year, and their crash risk is much higher than that of sober drivers.

Other drugs and illness

The law forbids having or using many drugs while on duty, and driving while affected by a controlled substance, amphetamines (“pep pills”, “uppers”, “bennies”), narcotics or anything else that can make you unsafe, which can include prescription and over-the-counter medicines such as cold remedies. Read the warning labels and follow your doctor’s instructions; stay away from illegal drugs.

Never take anything to hide fatigue: rest is the only cure. Alcohol can make other drugs much stronger, so the safest rule is not to mix drugs and driving at all. Drugs lead to crashes, arrests, fines, jail and the end of driving careers.

If you are too ill to drive safely, do not drive; in an emergency, drive only as far as the nearest safe place to stop.

Hazardous materials: what every driver must know

Every CDL holder must be able to recognize hazardous cargo and know whether they may haul it without the HazMat endorsement. Hazardous materials are products that put health, safety and property at risk during transport, and the rules aim to contain the product (safe packaging, and safe loading and unloading of bulk tanks), communicate the risk (shipping papers and diamond-shaped labels warn dock workers and drivers), and keep drivers and equipment safe.

After a crash you might be unable to tell responders what you carry, so the shipping papers must be easy to find: in a pouch on the driver’s door, in plain view within reach while you drive, or on the driver’s seat when you are out of the vehicle.

Placards on the outside of the vehicle tell others which hazard class it carries. A vehicle that must be placarded needs at least four identical placards (front, rear and both sides), readable from all four directions and at least 9.84 inches (250 mm) square, set on a point like a diamond. Identification numbers are four-digit codes, after the letters UN or NA, that first responders look up in the Emergency Response Guidebook (ERG); one number may cover several chemicals.

There are nine hazard classes: explosives (such as ammunition, dynamite and fireworks), gases (propane, oxygen, helium), flammable liquids (gasoline, acetone), flammable solids (matches), oxidizers (ammonium nitrate, hydrogen peroxide), poisons (pesticides, arsenic), radioactive materials (uranium, plutonium), corrosives (hydrochloric acid, battery fluid) and miscellaneous hazardous materials (formaldehyde, asbestos). ORM-D materials such as hair spray or charcoal and combustible liquids such as fuel oil and lighter fluid have no class number.

The endorsement rule

Not every vehicle carrying hazardous materials needs placards, and you may drive one that does not without the endorsement. A vehicle that needs placards requires the HazMat endorsement, earned with a knowledge test on Section 9 of the manual (see the HazMat lesson). Driving a vehicle that needs placards without the endorsement is a crime: you will be cited and not allowed to continue, and after a crash, emergency crews would not know what you carry. If you are not sure whether placards are needed, ask your employer.

Separately, the tank endorsement is needed for any vehicle built to carry liquids or gases in tanks of more than 119 gallons each and 1,000 gallons or more in total, permanently or temporarily attached, whether or not the product is hazardous.

HazMat drivers must also know which products may be loaded together; if in doubt, ask your employer and check the regulations before loading.

Cargo safety

Badly loaded or loose cargo endangers you and everyone around you: it can fall into traffic, hurt you in a sudden stop or crash, damage an overloaded vehicle, and make it harder to steer. Even if someone else loads and secures it, you are responsible for inspecting it, recognizing overloads and poor balance, making sure it is secured, and checking that it does not block your view ahead or to the sides or your access to emergency equipment.

Inspecting cargo

Your pre-trip inspection includes making sure the cargo is within the weight the truck can carry, evenly balanced and tied down. Then:

  • Look at the load and its securement again before you have driven 50 miles, and fix whatever has loosened.
  • Check again after 3 hours or 150 miles of driving, and after every break.

Weight, securement, covering rules and the roads big vehicles may use differ between federal, state and local rules, so learn the rules where you will drive.

Weight terms

  • GVWR (gross vehicle weight rating): the loaded weight the manufacturer sets for a single vehicle.
  • GCWR (gross combination weight rating): the manufacturer’s rating for the power unit and its towed units together.
  • Axle weight: what one axle or set of axles puts on the ground.
  • Tire load: the most weight a tire can safely carry at a given pressure, shown on the sidewall.
  • Suspension and coupling device ratings: manufacturers rate suspensions for weight, and coupling devices for the weight they can pull or carry.

Legal limits and overloading

States set maximums for GVWR, GCWR and axle weights. Axle limits often come from a bridge formula, which allows less weight on axles that are closer together, to protect bridges and roads. An overloaded truck steers, brakes and controls speed poorly: it crawls up hills, runs away on downgrades, needs longer to stop, and can overwork its brakes until they fail. In bad weather or in the mountains, even the legal maximum may be too much.

Center of gravity and balance

The higher the center of gravity (cargo piled high, or heavy items on top), the easier the vehicle tips over, especially in curves or when swerving. Keep the load as low as possible, with the heaviest items under the lightest.

Balance matters too. Too much weight on the steering axle makes steering hard and can damage the axle and tires; too little (weight shifted too far back) leaves too little grip to steer safely. Too little weight on the drive axles means poor traction, spinning wheels and, in bad weather, a truck that cannot keep moving. On flatbeds, poor loading also makes it more likely the load shifts sideways or falls off.

Securing cargo

  • Blocking is fitted tightly against the front, back or sides of the cargo and fastened to the deck to stop it sliding. Bracing runs from high up on the cargo down to the floor or across to the walls.
  • Tie-downs keep cargo on flatbeds and open trailers from shifting or falling, and help in closed vans too. Use the right type and strength: the combined working load limit of the securement system must be at least one-half of the weight of what it holds. Use proper ropes, straps, chains and tensioners (winches, ratchets, clinching parts), attached correctly to hooks, bolts, rails or rings. Use at least one tie-down for every ten feet of cargo, and never fewer than two, however small the load. Heavy metal loads have special rules.
  • Header boards (“headache racks”) protect you from the cargo in a crash or emergency stop. Keep the front-end structure in good condition so it stops the cargo moving forward.
  • Covers protect people from spilled cargo (required in many states) and protect the cargo from the weather. Check them in your mirrors: a flapping cover can tear loose, expose the cargo and block your view or someone else’s.
  • Sealed and containerized loads: containers that also travel by rail or ship may lock onto a special frame or ride on a flatbed, and must be secured like any other cargo. You cannot inspect a sealed load, but you must still make sure it does not put you over the gross and axle weight limits.

Worked example: counting tie-downs. The rule is at least one tie-down for every ten feet of cargo, and never fewer than two. A small crate only a few feet long still needs two tie-downs. A load 30 feet long needs at least three. Each tie-down must also be the right type and strength, because the working load limits of the whole system added together must reach at least half the weight of the cargo.

Loads that need special care

  • Dry bulk tanks have a high center of gravity and a load that can shift: go slowly through curves and sharp turns.
  • Hanging meat in a refrigerated trailer is unstable and top-heavy: take ramps and sharp curves slowly.
  • Livestock move around and lean in curves, raising the rollover risk. With a partial load, use false bulkheads to keep the animals together.
  • Oversized loads (too long, wide or heavy) need special permits and are often limited to certain hours. They may need “wide load” signs, flashing lights, flags, a police escort or pilot vehicles.

Easy to mix up

Looks alike How they differ How to tell them apart
.04% and “any detectable alcohol” At .04% or more it is illegal to drive a CMV, with the loss of your CDL for at least one year on a first offense. Any detectable amount below .04% puts you out of service for 24 hours. One is a violation with a license penalty. The other is a short, immediate stop.
One year, three years and for life A first offense costs at least one year, or at least three years in a vehicle placarded for hazardous materials. A second offense, or a felony with controlled substances, is for life. A placarded vehicle makes the first-offense loss three years instead of one. A second offense ends the CDL.
Beer, wine and liquor One standard drink is 12 ounces of beer at 5%, 5 ounces of wine at 12% or a 1.5-ounce shot of 80 proof liquor. They all hold the same alcohol, so “just a beer” is still a drink.
Placard and identification number and shipping papers Placards on the vehicle show the hazard class. The four-digit UN or NA number is looked up in the Emergency Response Guidebook. Shipping papers must be easy to find after a crash. Placard: outside, what class. Number: what chemical. Papers: easy for responders to find.
HazMat endorsement and tank endorsement HazMat is required for any vehicle that needs placards. The tank endorsement is for tanks over 119 gallons each and 1,000 gallons or more in total, whether or not the product is hazardous. Placards point to HazMat. Tank size points to tank. A vehicle can need both.
A hazard class number and no number The nine classes are numbered. ORM-D materials and combustible liquids have no class number. If it is hair spray, charcoal, fuel oil or lighter fluid, expect no number.
GVWR and GCWR GVWR is the loaded weight rating for a single vehicle. GCWR is the rating for the power unit and its towed units together. Single vehicle or combination: that is the letter C in the middle.
Axle weight and tire load Axle weight is what one axle or set of axles puts on the road. Tire load is the most a tire can carry at a given pressure. Axle: the road side. Tire: the sidewall.
Blocking and bracing Blocking is fitted tightly against the cargo and fastened to the deck so it cannot slide. Bracing runs from high on the cargo to the floor or across to the walls. Blocking stops sliding along the floor. Bracing holds the cargo up high.
Header board and cover A header board protects you from cargo moving forward in a crash or hard stop. A cover keeps cargo from spilling on others and shields it from weather. One guards you. The other guards everyone else and the load.
Within 50 miles and 3 hours or 150 miles The first cargo check is within 50 miles. Later checks come after 3 hours or 150 miles of driving, and after every break. First look early. Then keep checking on a rhythm.

Remember for the test

  • BAC .04% or more: illegal in a CMV. Any detectable amount under .04%: 24 hours out of service.
  • First offense: CDL lost for at least one year (three years with a placarded load); second offense: for life.
  • Only time sobers you up. One drink = 12 oz of 5% beer = 5 oz of 12% wine = 1.5 oz of 80 proof liquor.
  • Placards required means HazMat endorsement required. Shipping papers: door pouch, in view within reach, or on the seat.
  • Cargo checks: within 50 miles, then every 3 hours or 150 miles and after every break.
  • Bridge formula: less weight allowed on axles close together.
  • Heaviest cargo at the bottom. Tie-downs: one per ten feet, at least two.
