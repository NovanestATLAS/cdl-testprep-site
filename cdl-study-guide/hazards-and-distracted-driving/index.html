CDL General Knowledge: Seeing Hazards, Distracted and Aggressive Drivers

How to spot trouble before it becomes an emergency: hazardous roads, the clues other drivers give away, the federal phone and texting rules with their penalties, and how to stay calm around aggressive drivers.

A hazard is any road condition or road user (driver, cyclist, pedestrian) that could become a danger. Seeing it early buys you the seconds you need to check your mirrors, signal and move over or slow down before it turns into an emergency. This third General Knowledge lesson follows Sections 2.8 to 2.10 of the CDL manual.

Picture a car ahead drifting toward a freeway exit and then braking hard: the driver may be unsure and swing back onto the highway. Spot that early and you have time to change lanes or slow down; miss it and you may be braking hard when the car cuts back in front of you. The more you drive, the better you get at reading clues like this.

Hazardous roads

  • Move-over laws. Officers, medical crews, firefighters and road workers are being struck at the roadside more and more often. Where these laws apply, slow down and, if traffic and safety allow, move to a lane that is not next to the stopped emergency vehicle or work zone. If you cannot change lanes safely, slow down and go by carefully.
  • Work zones bring narrow lanes, sharp turns, uneven pavement, distracted drivers and workers or machines in the way. Drive slowly, and flash your brake lights or turn on the four-way flashers so the traffic behind you knows.
  • Drop-offs. Where the pavement drops sharply at the edge, driving too close can tilt the vehicle so the top strikes signs or branches, and steering across the edge, off or back on, can be difficult.
  • Foreign objects can damage tires, rims and electrical or brake lines, or wedge between duals. A box or sack that looks empty may hold something heavy.
  • Off and on ramps. Posted ramp speeds may suit cars but not a big or heavily loaded vehicle. Ramps that curve downhill are the worst: the slope makes slowing hard, and braking while turning is risky. Get down to a safe speed before the ramp.

Drivers and people who are hazards

Blocked vision

People who cannot see you are a serious hazard: drivers of vans, loaded station wagons or cars with a blocked rear window, renters of moving trucks who are not used to the blind spots, and anyone with frosted or snow-covered windows. If you can see only the front or back of a vehicle coming out of an alley or blind intersection, its driver cannot see you either, so be ready to stop. Delivery drivers are often in a hurry and may step out or pull into your lane with their view blocked by packages or doors. Watch parked cars for people inside, brake or backup lights and exhaust that show one is about to move, and take care around a stopped bus, where riders may walk out in front of it or behind it without seeing you.

People with their attention elsewhere

  • Pedestrians and cyclists may walk or ride with their backs to traffic, wear headphones, or hide under hats and umbrellas in the rain while hurrying.
  • Distracted people cannot see you if they are looking elsewhere, and even one looking right at you may think they have the right of way.
  • Children move suddenly without checking traffic, especially when playing together. An ice cream truck means children may be close by.
  • People talking to each other, workers on or near the road, people fixing a broken-down vehicle (a raised hood or a jacked-up wheel is the clue), people at a crash scene and shoppers looking at stores are often not watching traffic. At crashes, passing drivers stare and vehicles may slow or stop suddenly.

Drivers whose behavior gives clues

  • Confused drivers change direction or stop without warning, often near interchanges and big intersections. Tourists are a common case: look for luggage on the roof and out-of-state plates, and for odd moves such as stopping mid-block, lane changes for no clear reason or backup lights suddenly coming on.
  • Slow drivers are a hazard; spot them early. Mopeds, farm and construction machinery often carry the slow-moving vehicle sign: a red triangle with an orange center.
  • Drivers signaling a turn may slow more than you expect, crawl into a driveway, or stop in the road if people block them; a driver turning left may wait for oncoming traffic.
  • Drivers in a hurry may pass you without enough room or cut in to avoid being stuck behind you, forcing you to brake.
  • Impaired drivers (drunk, drugged, ill or sleepy): watch for weaving or drifting, wheels dropping off the pavement or bumping a curb in a turn, stopping at a green light or waiting too long at a stop sign, an open window in cold weather, and sudden changes of speed. Be especially alert late at night.
  • Body language: drivers usually look where they are about to turn, and a look over the shoulder may mean a lane change. These clues are easiest to see on motorcyclists and cyclists.
  • Conflicts: you are in a conflict whenever you must change speed or direction to avoid someone. They happen at intersections, merges and ramps, where lanes end, and around slow or stalled traffic and crash scenes. Watch for other drivers in a conflict, because they may react suddenly.

Always have a plan

Looking for hazards is only useful if you plan what you would do. Each time you see one, think about the emergency it could become and decide on your way out, then be ready to act on that plan.

Distracted driving

A distraction is anything that takes your attention off driving. Inside the cab that includes talking with passengers, fiddling with the radio or the heater, snacking, drinking or smoking, looking at maps, picking up something you dropped, phones, CB radios, texting, navigation screens and other electronics, and daydreaming. Outside, it includes other traffic, a police stop or crash scene, the setting sun, objects on the road, construction and billboards.

Why it matters

  • In a large study of truck crashes, 8 percent happened while the truck driver was distracted by something outside the vehicle and 2 percent by something inside it.
  • Distracted driving kills about 5,500 people and injures an estimated 448,000 on U.S. roads each year.
  • A phone conversation, even hands-free, takes away about 39% of the brain power normally given to driving. Hands-free is not safer than hand-held from the point of view of distraction.
  • Distraction slows perception (you notice a hazard late, or never), delays decisions and leads to wrong inputs on the steering, accelerator or brakes. Reacting just a half-second slower doubles the number of crashes.

There are three kinds of distraction: physical (your hands leave the wheel or your eyes leave the road, for example to grab an object), mental (your mind elsewhere, such as an absorbing conversation), and both at once, such as phoning or texting, which is the most dangerous.

The phone rule

Federal rules restrict CMV drivers from using hand-held mobile phones, and carriers may not require or allow it. Using a hand-held phone means holding it with at least one hand to talk, dialing by pressing more than a single button, or leaving your normal seated, belted driving position to reach for it. If you use a phone while driving, it must be a hands-free phone close to you that you can work while staying in that position. Reaching unsafely for the phone breaks the rule even if you meant to use hands-free.

Dialing drivers are 6 times more likely to be involved in a crash, near-crash or unintended lane departure, and they look away from the road for 3.8 seconds on average. Moving at 55 mph (80.7 feet per second), that adds up to 306 feet driven blind, roughly a football field.

Worked example: turning seconds into feet. At 55 mph your vehicle covers 80.7 feet every second. Multiply that by the 3.8 seconds a dialing driver looks away and you get roughly 306 feet. Do the same with the 4.6 seconds of a text and you get roughly 371 feet. The method works for any glance: seconds away from the road times 80.7 gives the feet you travel unseen at 55 mph.

The texting rule

Texting means typing or reading text on an electronic device: messages, email, instant messages, web pages or any other text, sent now or saved for later. It is prohibited for CMV drivers, and carriers may not require or allow it. It combines physical and mental distraction and is even riskier than talking: texting drivers are 23.2 times more likely to have a safety-critical event, and each text takes the eyes off the road for an average of 4.6 seconds, 371 feet at 55 mph.

Penalties

For both hand-held phone use and texting, a driver convicted two or more times under state law is disqualified: 60 days for the second offense in a 3-year period, and 120 days once there are three or more in 3 years. Each violation can also bring a civil penalty of up to $2,750 for the driver, and an employer can face up to $11,000. Both rules have an emergency exception: you may use a hand-held phone or text when you need to reach the police or other emergency services.

How to avoid distraction

Before you drive, think through what could distract you and plan around it.

  • Turn off devices you do not need. Never type or read a text while driving.
  • Learn the vehicle’s controls before setting out, and set the mirrors, seat and climate controls before you move.
  • Preset radio stations, plan the route and program the GPS before departure.
  • Clear out loose objects and secure the cargo.
  • Do not read or write while driving, and avoid eating, drinking and smoking at the wheel; leave early so you have time to stop for a meal.
  • Avoid complicated or emotional conversations, and ask passengers to help you stay focused.

Watch for other distracted drivers too: vehicles drifting across or within their lane, speeds that keep changing, and drivers busy with phones, food, maps or their passengers. Give them plenty of room, keep a safe following distance, and take great care passing them, since they may drift in front of you.

Aggressive drivers and road rage

Aggressive driving is driving in a selfish, bold or pushy way with no regard for other people’s rights or safety, such as cutting from lane to lane without warning. Road rage goes further: driving with the intent to harm others, or physically attacking a driver or a vehicle. Crowded roads and tight schedules make both more common.

Don’t become one

  • Your mood before you start driving shapes how stress affects you, so calm down first; quiet music helps.
  • Give driving your full attention.
  • Plan realistic travel times that allow for traffic, construction and weather. If you are going to be late, accept it.
  • Give others the benefit of the doubt: whatever their reason for driving badly, it is not about you.
  • Slow down, keep a reasonable following distance, and do not drive slowly in the left lane.
  • Keep your hands on the wheel and make no gestures, not even a shake of the head.
  • Be courteous: if someone wants to get in front of you, let them.

When you meet one

  • Get out of the way. Leave your pride behind: do not speed up or hold your lane to challenge them.
  • Avoid eye contact and ignore their gestures.
  • Report them to the police with the vehicle description, plate number, location and direction of travel; call if you have a phone and can do it safely.
  • If that driver later crashes, stop a safe distance from the scene, wait for the police and tell them what you saw.

Easy to mix up

Looks alike How they differ How to tell them apart
Hand-held and hands-free phone use A hand-held phone is held in at least one hand to talk, dialed with more than a single button, or reached for by leaving your normal seated, belted position. A hands-free phone is within reach so you can use it from that position. The test is your position and your hands, not the brand of phone. Reaching unsafely for a hands-free phone still breaks the rule.
Phone rule and texting rule You may talk on a hands-free phone, though even that takes about 39% of the brainpower you give to driving. You may not text at all, whether you are sending or reading. Talking can be allowed if it is hands-free. Texting never is.
3.8 seconds and 4.6 seconds 3.8 seconds is the average eyes-off-road time for dialing, about 306 feet at 55 mph. 4.6 seconds is the average for texting, about 371 feet. Texting is the longer, riskier glance.
6 times and 23.2 times Dialing makes a crash, near-crash or lane departure 6 times more likely. Texting makes a safety-critical event 23.2 times more likely. Dialing is bad. Texting is far worse.
60 days and 120 days A second conviction within 3 years means 60 days of disqualification. A third or later conviction in that time means 120 days. Second offense, shorter. Third or more, twice as long.
Driver and employer penalties A driver can face a civil penalty of up to $2,750 per violation. An employer can face up to $11,000. The employer’s maximum is the larger figure, and carriers may not require or allow either behavior.
Aggressive driving and road rage Aggressive driving is selfish, bold or pushy driving with no regard for others, such as lane-cutting without warning. Road rage is intent to harm others, or an attack on a driver or vehicle. Aggressive driving ignores others. Road rage goes after them.
Hazard and emergency A hazard is a possible danger. It becomes an emergency when the danger is no longer just possible, for example when the car that was drifting cuts in front of you. Plan for the hazard, so it never becomes the emergency.

Remember for the test

  • A hazard seen early gives you time to plan an escape before it becomes an emergency.
  • Move over (or slow down) for stopped emergency vehicles and work zones.
  • Hand-held phone: only hands-free use, within reach, without leaving your seated, belted position. Texting: prohibited.
  • Disqualification: 60 days for a second conviction within 3 years, 120 days for a third or more; driver penalty up to $2,750.
  • Dialing: 3.8 seconds and 306 feet without looking at 55 mph. Texting: 4.6 seconds and 371 feet.
  • Aggressive driving puts others at risk; road rage means intent to harm.
