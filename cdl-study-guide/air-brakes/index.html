CDL Air Brakes Study Guide: How Air Brakes Work and How to Check Them

How an air brake system builds and stores pressure, what every gauge and warning means, the air brake checks you must know for the test, and how to brake safely on long downgrades.

Air brakes stop heavy trucks and buses with compressed air instead of brake fluid. They are reliable, but only when the driver keeps the system in good shape and uses it the right way. You need the air brakes knowledge test to drive a vehicle with air brakes; without it your license carries the L restriction. This lesson follows Section 5 of the CDL manual.

Three brake systems in one

An air brake system is really three systems that share parts:

  • Service brakes slow and stop you in normal driving, when you press the brake pedal.
  • Parking brakes hold the vehicle when you set the parking brake control.
  • Emergency brakes use parts of the other two to stop the vehicle if the system fails.

The parts you must know

Part What it does Remember
Air compressor Pumps air into the storage tanks. It is driven by the engine through gears or a v-belt. If it has its own oil supply, check the oil before driving.
Governor Switches the compressor on and off. It stops pumping at the cut-out pressure (around 125 psi) and starts again at the cut-in pressure (around 100 psi).
Air storage tanks Hold the compressed air. They hold enough air for several brake applications even if the compressor stops.
Tank drains Let out the water and oil that collect at the bottom of the tanks. Water can freeze and cause brake failure. Drain manual tanks completely at the end of each driving day.
Alcohol evaporator Adds alcohol to the air to fight ice in valves. Check and fill it every day in cold weather. You still drain the tanks.
Safety valve Protects the first tank from too much pressure. Usually opens at 150 psi. If it lets air out, something is wrong: have a mechanic fix it.
Brake pedal (foot or treadle valve) Pushing harder sends more air to the brakes. Fanning the pedal wastes air faster than the compressor can replace it.
Supply pressure gauge Shows the pressure in the tanks. Dual systems have two gauges or one gauge with two needles.
Application pressure gauge Shows how much air you are sending to the brakes (not on every vehicle). If you must press harder just to keep the same speed, your brakes are fading.
Low air pressure warning Red light, often with a buzzer, or a “wig wag” arm. Must come on before tank pressure drops below 55 psi. On large buses it often comes on at 80-85 psi.
Stop light switch Turns on the brake lights when air is applied. Warns the drivers behind you.

Foundation brakes at each wheel

The most common wheel brake is the s-cam drum brake. When you press the pedal, air enters the brake chamber and pushes a rod that moves the slack adjuster, which twists the camshaft. The S-shaped cam spreads the brake shoes against the inside of the drum, and the friction slows the wheel and makes heat. Wedge brakes and disc brakes do the same job with a wedge or a “power screw” and are less common. Every drum can take only so much heat: too much and the brakes stop working.

Front brake limiting valves

Some vehicles built before 1975 have a cab control marked “normal” and “slippery” that cuts air to the front brakes by half. That reduces stopping power. Front brakes are good in all conditions, so keep the control on “normal”.

Dual air brake systems

Most heavy vehicles have two separate air systems, primary and secondary, run by one set of controls. One usually brakes the rear axle or axles, the other the front axle (and maybe one rear axle). Both feed a trailer if there is one.

Before you drive, let the compressor build at least 100 psi in both systems. The low pressure warning should go off once both rise above the maker’s setting, which must be more than 55 psi. If the warning comes on while driving, stop and park safely right away: with one system low, either the front or the rear brakes are not working fully and you will need more distance to stop.

Spring brakes and the parking brake

Parking and emergency brakes have to work without air, because air pressure can slowly leak out, so they are applied by mechanical force. Most vehicles use spring brakes: strong springs held back by air pressure while you drive. Remove the air and the springs apply the brakes.

  • Spring brakes come fully on when pressure falls to between 20 and 45 psi (typically 20 to 30). Do not wait for that: when the low air warning comes on, stop while you still control the service brakes.
  • On newer vehicles the parking brake is a yellow, diamond-shaped knob. Pull it out to set the parking brakes, push it in to release them. Older vehicles may use a lever or a different knob.
  • Never press the brake pedal while the spring brakes are on. The spring force and the air force together can damage the brakes.
  • A modulating control valve lets you apply the spring brakes gradually, so you can still slow down if the service brakes fail.
  • Some buses have dual parking control valves with a separate tank that can release the spring brakes a few times, so the bus can still be moved in an emergency. Plan each move, because that air runs out.
  • Spring brakes only work well if the brakes are adjusted. Badly adjusted brakes weaken both the service and the parking brakes.

Antilock brakes (ABS)

ABS is required on air-braked truck tractors made from March 1, 1997 onward, and on other air-braked trucks, buses, trailers and converter dollies from March 1, 1998 onward. A computer senses that a wheel is about to lock and reduces the brake pressure on it.

  • A yellow malfunction lamp shows an ABS fault: on the dashboard for tractors, trucks and buses, and on the left side of a trailer. On newer vehicles it lights for a bulb check at start-up and then goes out.
  • If it stays on, or comes on while you drive, you may have lost ABS at one or more wheels. Your normal brakes still work: drive normally and have it serviced soon.
  • ABS does not make your brakes stronger or weaker and does not always shorten stopping distance. Its value is that you can keep steering while braking hard.
  • With ABS on only the tractor or only the trailer you still gain control. Brake the same way whichever unit has it, and ease off if the trailer starts to swing out or the tractor starts to jackknife.
  • If every axle of your truck or combination has working ABS, you may brake fully in an emergency stop.

Inspecting air brakes

Air brakes add checks to the seven-step vehicle inspection from Section 2.

Under the hood (step 2): if the compressor is belt-driven, check that the belt is in good condition and tight.

Walk-around (step 5):

  • Slack adjusters. Park on level ground, chock the wheels and release the parking brakes. Wearing gloves, pull hard on each slack adjuster you can reach. If you can move it more than about an inch at the point where it joins the push rod, it probably needs adjustment. Roadside inspectors find brakes out of adjustment more often than any other problem.
  • Vehicles built since 1994 have automatic slack adjusters. They still must be checked, and if one is out of adjustment the vehicle should go to a repair shop: adjusting it by hand usually hides a mechanical problem and should only be an emergency measure.
  • Drums, linings and hoses. No drum crack longer than half the width of the friction area. Linings must not be loose, soaked in oil or grease, or dangerously thin. Hoses to the brake chambers must not be cut or worn.

Final air brake check (step 7), done instead of the hydraulic brake check:

  1. Governor cut-out and cut-in. At fast idle, pumping should stop at about 125 psi. Then fan the brake pedal to lower the pressure: pumping should start again at about 100 psi. Check the maker’s numbers.
  2. Air leakage, brakes released. With a full system (typically 125 psi), stop the engine, release the parking brake and time the drop: less than 2 psi in one minute for a single vehicle, less than 3 psi for a combination.
  3. Air leakage, brakes applied. With pressure at governor cut-out, engine off, parking brake (and tractor protection valve) released, hold the brake pedal down for one minute. The drop must not exceed 3 psi for a single vehicle or 4 psi for a combination. More than that: find and fix the leak before you drive.
  4. Low pressure warning. Engine off, power on, fan the pedal. The warning must come on before tank pressure falls below 55 psi.
  5. Spring brakes come on automatically. Keep fanning. The parking brake valve (and on a tractor-trailer the tractor protection valve) should pop out at the maker’s setting, between 20 and 45 psi.
  6. Pressure build-up. At operating rpm a dual system should go from 85 to 100 psi within 45 seconds. Older single systems (before 1975) typically need 50 to 90 psi within 3 minutes at 600-900 rpm.
  7. Parking brake and service brakes. Set the parking brake and pull gently against it in a low gear: it must hold. Then roll forward at about 5 mph and brake firmly. Note any pulling to one side, odd feel or slow stop.

Using air brakes on the road

Normal and emergency stops

For a normal stop, press the pedal with steady, controlled pressure. With a manual transmission, keep the clutch out until the engine is near idle.

In an emergency, keep the vehicle straight and able to steer:

  • Controlled braking: brake as firmly as possible while keeping the wheels from locking, and keep steering movements small. If the wheels lock or you need to steer more, release and brake again as soon as you can.
  • Stab braking: apply the brakes fully, release when the wheels lock, and apply fully again once they roll. Wheels can take up to one second to start rolling again; braking before they do keeps the vehicle from straightening out.

Brake lag and stopping distance

Air needs time to travel through the lines, about half a second or more. So the total stopping distance with air brakes adds up four parts: perception distance, reaction distance, brake lag distance and braking distance. At 55 mph on dry pavement brake lag adds about 32 feet, and the total stopping distance is over 450 feet.

Brake fade on long downgrades

Brakes turn speed into heat. Overused brakes overheat: the linings lose friction and the drums expand away from the shoes, until the vehicle may not stop at all. Brakes out of adjustment stop working first and leave the others to overheat.

On a long or steep grade, the engine does most of the work and the brakes only help. Get into the right low gear first, then use this technique:

  1. Brake just hard enough to feel a clear slowdown.
  2. When you are about 5 mph below your safe speed, release the brakes. One application lasts about three seconds.
  3. When you are back up to your safe speed, repeat.

Example: with a safe speed of 40 mph, wait until you reach 40, brake down to 35, release, and repeat to the bottom of the grade.

Low air pressure and parking

  • If the low air warning comes on, stop and park as soon as it is safe. Spring brakes come on between 20 and 45 psi, and they do not work on every axle: a heavy load needs a long distance to stop, and a light load or slippery road can make the vehicle skid.
  • Set the parking brakes whenever you park, except when the brakes are very hot (they can be damaged) or very wet in freezing weather (they can freeze). Then chock the wheels on level ground, let hot brakes cool, and dry wet brakes by braking lightly in a low gear.
  • Never leave the vehicle without setting the parking brakes or chocking the wheels.
  • Without automatic drains, empty the air tanks every working day when you finish.

Key numbers to remember

Number What it means
About 125 psi Governor cut-out: the compressor stops pumping
About 100 psi Governor cut-in: pumping starts again; minimum to build before driving a dual system
150 psi Usual opening pressure of the safety valve
55 psi Low air warning must come on before tank pressure falls below this
20-45 psi Range where spring brakes come on (typically 20-30)
2 / 3 psi in a minute Most leakage allowed with brakes released (single / combination)
3 / 4 psi in a minute Most leakage allowed with the brake pedal held down (single / combination)
85 to 100 psi in 45 seconds Minimum build-up rate of a dual system
About 1 inch Slack adjuster movement that means adjustment is probably needed
About 32 feet Brake lag distance at 55 mph
5 mph below safe speed When to release the brakes on a downgrade

Common mistakes

  • Waiting for the spring brakes to stop you instead of stopping as soon as the low air warning comes on.
  • Pressing the brake pedal while the spring brakes are on.
  • Fanning the brake pedal, which wastes air.
  • Riding the brakes downhill instead of choosing a low gear first.
  • Setting the parking brakes on very hot brakes, or on wet brakes in freezing weather.
  • Adjusting an automatic slack adjuster by hand instead of getting the brakes repaired.
